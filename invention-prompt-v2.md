# FinanceCraft — Invention Prompt (v3, Track 2 / "The Hypothetical")

*The counterpart to the Research Prompt. Use this for POV scenarios, thought experiments, and any invented-scenario episode. Replace `[PREMISE]` and `[MECHANIC]`. Fill `[SKELETON]` if you know it — Part 8 only fires if you do. Do not use the Track 1 research prompt for these.*

---

I'm building a fictional scenario episode for a financial documentary channel. Rough premise: **[PREMISE]**. The real financial or physical mechanic I want it to teach: **[MECHANIC]**. Skeleton this is for (see `SKELETON_LIBRARY.md`, e.g. A5, A6, A9, A10): **[SKELETON]**.

This is invented, not reported. Do not search for real events to cite. Do not give me studies, statistics, or academic sources. The only thing that must be factually correct is the mechanic itself.

## Register

Plain spoken English. Short sentences. Concrete nouns. No analysis, no framing paragraphs, no summary. Where I ask for a list, give a bare list.

## The discipline this version adds: don't let the chain wander off the premise

Every beat below has to still be visibly about **[PREMISE]** to a first-time viewer. It's easy to follow a mechanism to its most technically interesting place and end up making a video about something else entirely wearing this premise's title — a "what if the feeds went dark" video that becomes, by the midpoint, a video about port demurrage schedules and OAuth token lifecycles. That happened here before. The fix isn't to avoid going deep — the $1 Trillion benchmark goes deep into yachts, grain markets, and stock exchanges — it's that every domain it visits is still, transparently, about *spending the money*. The throughline never breaks even as the domains change. Hold your chains to that same test: **does this beat still feel like it's about [PREMISE], or has [PREMISE] become a forgotten trigger for a different topic?**

## PART 1 — Five premises, not one

Give me five versions of this scenario, and make at least two of them uncomfortable — past the point of reasonable. For each, one line only.

Use these angles:
1. **The constraint twist** — add a rule that makes the obvious path impossible
2. **The wrong protagonist** — the position nobody picks to tell it from
3. **The inversion** — the recovery instead of the collapse; the person who called it early and got punished
4. **The absurd literalisation** — take the abstraction and give it physical weight and a location
5. **The compressed clock** — force a decade into a week, or stretch an instant across a year

Then: which is strongest, and one sentence on why each of the other four is weaker.

## PART 2 — The contract

The scenario's rules, stated the way a legal notice would state them:
- What just happened (one sentence)
- The clock — how long
- The penalty — what is lost when it runs out
- The rules — what is and isn't allowed inside the scenario
- **The adversary** — the thing working against the protagonist *while they act*. Compounding interest, spreading failure, a closing window. It must be expressible as a number that moves.

All five must fit in 80 spoken words. Write them that way.

**If this is a countdown/ledger format (A5), give me the reconciled numbers now, not later.** State the adversary's rate plainly (e.g. "$X burned per day"), then compute the running figure at 3-4 checkpoints across the scenario's clock **by that rate**, showing the arithmetic (`start − rate × days = figure`). Every checkpoint must actually follow from the stated rate. A ledger with numbers that don't reconcile with their own stated cause is the single most common failure in this format, and it's a research failure, not a scripting one — it has to be caught here.

## PART 3 — The world kit

Invented, but specific. Vague is the failure mode here, not fabrication.

1. **Three named places**, each with one distinguishing physical feature
2. **Three recurring objects** the viewer sees more than once and learns to read
3. **One recurring human figure** who says almost nothing and is never explained
4. **One absurd-feeling detail per act** — the kind a viewer repeats to a friend

## PART 4 — Third-order consequences

For the four biggest beats, push the chain three steps past the obvious:

> first order (what everyone predicts) → second order → **third order (the beat worth filming), and the one-line test: how would a viewer still know this is about [PREMISE]?**

Worked example, staying on-theme throughout (the premise: a sudden ban on a country's cash currency):
*people can't pay for anything in cash → shopkeepers refuse goods without an app they don't have → a street vendor invents a paper IOU system scrawled in a school notebook, backed by nothing but her own name, because the thing missing was never the money, it was a way to promise it.*
Every step of that chain is still recognizably about *paying for things*. Compare the failure version: a premise about paying for things drifting into a chain about warehouse authentication protocols and shipping-container law — technically downstream, but the viewer has stopped watching a video about payment three steps ago.

Stop at first order and I have a list of things the viewer already assumed. I want scenes. But stop the chain the moment it stops being about [PREMISE], even if a more interesting mechanism is one step further down it — save that mechanism for a different episode with its own premise.

## PART 5 — The mechanic, verified (the only research in this prompt)

Confirm the mechanic works the way I've described. Specifically:
- Does the math actually behave this way? Show it.
- What realistic figures anchor it — real rates, real thresholds, real physical limits, real timelines?
- Where does my premise get the mechanic **wrong**? Say so directly.

If the mechanic is broken, the scenario is worthless no matter how good the story is. Be blunt here.

## PART 6 — The quotable beat

One moment. The single thing a viewer describes to someone else the next day. If you can't identify one, say so — that means the premise isn't ready.

## PART 7 — Where this gets boring

Be honest: which parts of this scenario are predictable, which beats have been done, and where would a viewer stop watching?

## Output rules

- Bare lists where lists are asked for
- Invented people, companies, places and dialogue are expected and wanted — be specific, not careful
- Real companies, products and institutions may be named as things the scenario *acts upon* (buying them, losing access to them). Never invent quotes, motives, or wrongdoing for a real entity.
- No studies, no citations, no statistics — except in Part 5
- "Not sure" is an acceptable answer; invented certainty is not
