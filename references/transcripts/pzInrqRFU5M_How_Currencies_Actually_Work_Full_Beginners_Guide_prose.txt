Simple. The interesting part is why people suddenly want more or less of a currency. There are four big engines behind that. Engine one, interest rates.

When a central bank raises interest rates, it becomes more attractive to hold that currency, because your money earns more sitting in that country's bank accounts and bonds. Investors worldwide notice, and money starts

Cut rates, and the opposite happens.

Money flows out looking for a better return elsewhere.

This dynamic even has its own trading strategy built around it called the carry trade. Borrow money in a currency with near zero interest rates, convert it into a currency paying much more, and pocket the difference.

It sounds like free money right up until the exchange rate itself swings against you and wipes out the gain in a single bad week.

Engine two, inflation. If a country's currency is losing purchasing power fast at home, prices rising, your money buying less. That weakness tends to show up abroad, too. Nobody wants to hold a currency that's quietly losing value

Lower relative inflation generally makes a currency more attractive over time.

Engine three, trade balances. If a country imports far more than it exports, it constantly needs to convert its own currency into foreign currency to pay for all that stuff coming in.

That steady selling pressure weakens the currency over time.

Export-heavy countries see the reverse.

Engine four, speculation and capital flows. This is the one that makes currencies move minute by minute.

Traders and institutions aren't just reacting to what's happening. They're betting on what's about to happen.
