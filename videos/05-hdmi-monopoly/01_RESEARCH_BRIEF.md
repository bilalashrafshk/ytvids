## VERDICT ON THE WORKING THESIS (read first)

- **"Multi-billion-dollar" is dropped.** About 14 billion devices at $0.04–$0.15 comes to roughly **$0.6–0.7 billion likely, $0.56–2.1 billion outer range**, over 23 years. That's arithmetic on sourced inputs, so it must be said on camera as "roughly" and "by our estimate". [UNCERTAIN]
- **"Every cable" is dropped.** A cable sold inside a box with a TV doesn't pay; the TV maker pays once. A cable sold directly to consumers does. [SECONDARY]
- **"$0.04" is the lowest tier, not the price.** Base $0.15; $0.05 with the logo; $0.04 with logo and HDCP. The copy-protection lock costs extra on top (see Step 3).
- **The strong angle is control, not money.** A cheap fee, a lock nobody can skip, a trademark, and a court that upheld it.
- **DisplayPort is not free.** It is marketed royalty-free by its standards body, but a patent pool asks $0.20 per product, which is *more* than HDMI's lowest tier. That kills the "free escape route" beat and gives a better one.

## PART 1 — What people get wrong, or don't know

- Most people think an HDMI cable is a standard part like a wall socket: nobody owns it, nobody charges for it.
- The truth: HDMI is a private licence. A maker signs a contract with HDMI Licensing Administrator (HDMI LA) and pays an annual fee plus a fee on every device. [PRIMARY: hdmi.org confirms adopters "pay annual fees and royalties"; fee figures below are SECONDARY]
- The fee is tiny per device, so it's invisible. Nobody sees the bill.
- The bigger fact almost nobody knows: the contract also covers the trademark and the patents, so a maker without it can't legally build a compliant port or call it HDMI. [PRIMARY, HDMI LA licensed-products notice, Aug 13, 2010]

## PART 2 — The mechanism, in stages

**Step 1 — Rivals build one shared connector (2002).**
- Founders: Hitachi, Matsushita (Panasonic), Philips, Silicon Image, Sony, Thomson, Toshiba. [PRIMARY, hdmi.org press release; SECONDARY, Wikipedia]
- Today's founder list on hdmi.org is Philips, Lattice, Maxell, Panasonic, Sony, Toshiba, Vantiva. Thomson is now Vantiva. [PRIMARY for the list; SECONDARY for the name change. Say "the founders, under their current names"; don't map the rest in script.]
- Development began April 16, 2002; version 1.0 released December 9, 2002. [PRIMARY, hdmi.org]
- Goal: one connector compatible with DVI, replacing VGA, DVI and RCA leads. [SECONDARY]
- Analogy: seven rival shops agreeing on one shared key for every door.
- If removed: no single connector, so a mess of older cables.

**Step 2 — They add a copy-protection lock so film studios say yes.**
- Intel contributed HDCP, the copy-protection system. [SECONDARY, Wikipedia]
- Backers: Fox, Universal, Warner Bros., Disney, DirecTV, EchoStar, CableLabs. [SECONDARY, Wikipedia]
- Analogy: a bouncer who only lets in people with a licensed badge.
- If removed: studios don't put films on it, so it doesn't matter.

**Step 3 — A device with no licensed key can't show protected content, and the keys cost money.**
- Each HDCP device holds 40 keys; two devices swap key values to agree a shared secret. A device without valid licensed keys "cannot authenticate" and cannot receive the content. [SECONDARY, Wikipedia]
- HDCP is licensed by Digital Content Protection LLC, an Intel subsidiary. [SECONDARY, Wikipedia]
- **HDCP fees, from DCP's own licence agreement (revised March 15, 2024, review copy): annual fee $15,000 a year; device keys cost $15,000 per 1,000,000 keys, $7,500 per 100,000, $3,000 per 10,000.** That's $0.015, $0.075 and $0.30 a key. Small makers pay the most per unit. [PRIMARY, Procedural Appendix §1.1–1.2]
- Analogy: a TV without the badge is a locked room with the film on the other side.
- If removed: any maker could build a port with no contract.

**Step 4 — The contract puts a price on the port, and a patent licence comes with it.**
- Annual fee: $10,000 a year (more than 10,000 units); or $5,000 a year plus $1 per unit (10,000 or fewer). [SECONDARY: Wikipedia, and consistent across the retailer/distributor explainers; HDMI LA keeps the price sheet behind its adopter extranet]
- Per-unit royalty: $0.15 base; $0.05 with the HDMI logo; $0.04 with the logo and HDCP. [SECONDARY: same]
- Per device, not per port, chip or connector. [SECONDARY]
- The logo discount is real: HDMI LA's 2022 guidelines say makers get "a discounted royalty rate under the Adopter Agreement" for using the stylised logo on products, documentation and promotional materials. The wordmark or port design alone doesn't qualify. [PRIMARY, Adopted Trademark and Logo Usage Guidelines, rev. May 3, 2022]
- The Adopter Agreement grants a patent licence to the HDMI specification's "Necessary Claims" (Section 2), but only for compliant "Licensed Products", defined as "a Cable, Component, Connector, Repeater, Source or Sink". The licence "shall not extend to any part or function of a product in which a Licensed Product is incorporated that is not itself a Licensed Product." [PRIMARY, hdmi.org "Clarification on HDMI Licensed Products", Aug 13, 2010]
- Who pays for cables: a cable sold to an adopter and put in a TV doesn't pay a royalty; the TV maker pays on the final product. A cable sold directly to consumers is subject to a royalty. [SECONDARY]
- Analogy: a toll that gets cheaper the more you advertise the toll company.
- If removed: no income, no enforcement.

**Step 5 — HDMI LA acts for the founders, and enforces in court.**
- HDMI LA is "the agent appointed by the HDMI Founders to license earlier HDMI Specifications" and "the agent appointed by the HDMI Forum to license Version 2.2." [PRIMARY, hdmi.org founders page]
- The HDMI Forum was set up by the founders on October 25, 2011 and owns specifications from 2.0. [SECONDARY, Wikipedia]
- Availink, a chip maker, argued HDMI LA and its founders "violated antitrust and unfair competition laws because they possessed market power in markets that use the HDMI specification, and restrained competition … through specific terms in their adopter agreement." [SECONDARY, Analysis Group, expert for HDMI LA]
- December 31, 2025: the federal judge in N.D. California granted summary judgment for HDMI LA. The court found "(1) the Adopter Agreement did not harm competition; (2) Availink did not suffer antitrust injury; and (3)" even if it had, its injuries didn't arise from the domestic effects. All Availink counterclaims were dismissed. [SECONDARY, Constantine Cannon (HDMI LA's lawyers)]
- HDMI LA's own statement: "The license terms unambiguously required Availink to pay royalties and annual fees for its use of the HDMI Specification and prohibited the continued use of the specification following termination." [PRIMARY, hdmi.org blog, Dec 31, 2025]
- Court held royalties must be paid by the chip maker on ICs sold to non-adopters, "even though its semiconductor products were incorporated into products sold to end-users." [SECONDARY, Constantine Cannon; HDMI LA's summary]
- One week before trial Availink agreed to a **$14 million judgment** and signed a new Adopter Agreement. [SECONDARY, Constantine Cannon; PRIMARY for the settlement, HDMI LA statement]
- Availink was licensed for about three years before termination. [PRIMARY, HDMI LA statement]
- Other cases: HDMI LA v. Chunghsin (Chinese companies with counterfeit HDMI marks at CES). [PRIMARY, court listing; outcome not confirmed, so mention only as "sued"]
- If removed: the contract is only a suggestion.

## PART 4 — Texture (raw lists)

1. **Physical objects:** the port on the back of a TV; a bag of HDMI cables from a drawer; the HDMI logo on a box; a laptop with one port and a dongle; a game console box.
2. **Everyday moment:** plugging a console or streaming stick into the TV and it just works. The bill was paid before the TV left the factory.
3. **Recognisable numbers:** four cents is less than a gumball; the TV costs hundreds of dollars; about 14 billion devices.
4. **A real episode where the lock broke:** on September 14, 2010 a master key for HDCP surfaced; Intel confirmed the breach on September 16, believing it was reverse-engineered, and threatened legal action against anyone producing circumvention devices. [SECONDARY, Wikipedia]
5. **Counter-intuitive fact:** the fee is per device, so a TV with five ports pays once, not five times. [SECONDARY]
6. **Joke-shaped detail 1:** the logo discount means a maker pays less for advertising the toll company. [PRIMARY]
7. **Joke-shaped detail 2:** since 2023, a port can be sold as "HDMI 2.1" even if it only does the older HDMI 2.0 things. HDMI LA's answer, as reported: HDMI 2.0 "no longer exists" and its features are a subset of 2.1, with all the new features optional. [SECONDARY, TFTCentral]
8. **Joke-shaped detail 3:** the "free" rival isn't free either. DisplayPort's patent pool asks $0.20 a product, and its list of patent owners includes Hitachi Maxell, Philips, Silicon Image and Sony, the same names as the HDMI founders. [SECONDARY, Via LA brief; Wikipedia]
9. **Small makers pay more:** a company buying 10,000 HDCP keys pays $0.30 a key; a giant buying a million pays $0.015. [PRIMARY]
10. **A device that strips the lock:** the HDFury Integral; Warner Bros. sued on December 31, 2015 and settled in May 2016 after the maker argued it downgraded rather than stripped protection. [SECONDARY, Wikipedia]

## PART 5 — The escalation case

The escalation is from cents to control, not to a bigger number.

1. **A cable and four cents.** One cheap cable, one tiny fee. (Living room.)
2. **The multiplier.** About 900 million devices in 2017 alone, at $0.04–$0.15, so tens to low hundreds of millions in one year. Across 14 billion devices, about $0.6–2 billion. Real money, not a Big Tech figure.
3. **The lock.** A TV without a licensed key can't show protected content, so skipping the contract isn't cheaper, it's useless. The keys are another fee, cheapest for the biggest makers.
4. **The courtroom.** A chip maker called the arrangement an illegal monopoly. The judge ruled for HDMI LA on December 31, 2025, and it paid $14 million.
5. **The gate.** The Forum controls the newest specification. AMD's engineers spent months trying to ship an open-source HDMI 2.1 driver for Linux, and reported in 2024 that the Forum rejected it. "At this time an open source HDMI 2.1 implementation is not possible without running afoul of the HDMI Forum requirements." Linux users lose 4K at 120 Hz and 5K at 240 Hz over HDMI 2.1. [SECONDARY: The Register, Mar 2, 2024; Phoronix, Tom's Hardware via search. The Forum's own statement was not found; do not quote the Forum, quote AMD.]
6. **The kicker.** AMD's answer to gamers: use DisplayPort. Which has its own $0.20 patent-pool fee in some countries. [SECONDARY]

## PART 6 — Where this gets boring

- **The fee tiers.** Three rates and two conditions turns into a table fast. Fix: say "four cents" and treat $0.15 and $0.05 as the price you pay if you don't play along.
- **HDCP.** "Digital key exchange" is a lecture. Fix: a bouncer with a badge.
- **The two organisations.** HDMI LA (licensing, trademark, enforcement) and the HDMI Forum (specifications) are easy to confuse. Fix: introduce only the Forum late, in the driver fight.
- **The antitrust case.** Legal detail is dull. Fix: one sentence and the $14 million.
- **The money.** The honest number is smaller than the title implies. The script must not inflate it; the turn is that the size of the fee doesn't matter.

## PART 7A — A3 kit (Draft A)

**Causal chain (dated):**
1. Apr 16, 2002 — seven rivals start building one connector. [PRIMARY]
2. Dec 9, 2002 — version 1.0 released, with Intel's copy-protection lock so studios and satellite providers back it. [PRIMARY/SECONDARY]
3. Makers sign the Adopter Agreement and pay the annual fee and the per-device royalty, cheaper with the logo and HDCP. [SECONDARY/PRIMARY]
4. A device without HDCP keys can't show protected content, so skipping the contract fails. [SECONDARY]
5. May 2006 — DisplayPort arrives for computers; HDMI is already in living rooms. [SECONDARY]
6. Sep 2010 — the copy-protection master key leaks; Intel threatens legal action. [SECONDARY]
7. Oct 25, 2011 — the founders create the HDMI Forum; it owns specifications from 2.0. [SECONDARY]
8. Dec 31, 2025 — a court rules for HDMI LA against a chip maker's antitrust challenge; $14 million follows. [PRIMARY/SECONDARY]
9. 2023–2024 — the Forum turns down AMD's open-source driver. [SECONDARY]

**The loop (numbered, for the midpoint):**
1. Studios won't let films play on unprotected screens.
2. So every screen needs the lock.
3. So every screen maker needs the contract.
4. So every screen pays, and the group behind it controls the rules.

**Everyday analogy for the hardest step:** a club with one door and a bouncer; anyone can build a nicer door, but the party is inside.

## PART 7B — A8 kit (Draft B)

- **The prior:** an HDMI cable is a generic cable that nobody owns.
- **Mechanism layers (smallest to biggest):**
  1. The fee: a few cents per device, paid by the maker.
  2. The lock: without it, the device can't show protected content, so nobody can skip the contract.
  3. The gate: the trademark, the patents and the newest specification are controlled by the group; open-source developers were told no.
- **Nested paradox:** the fee is so small it looks harmless, yet it's the thing that makes the arrangement stick, because it's cheaper to pay than to fight. Resolution: Availink fought and paid $14 million. [PRIMARY/SECONDARY]
- **Physical stake:** what disappears if you skip the contract is your ability to sell a working TV, or, for Linux users, the 4K 120 Hz mode on an AMD card over HDMI 2.1. [SECONDARY]

## PART 8 — Honest complication (Q8 slot)

- One universal port is why plugging in a TV just works; the licence pays for testing, the trademark and enforcement. [HDMI LA's own framing of its purpose; not independently sourced. Attribute it: "HDMI LA says…"]
- The fee is a few cents on a device that costs hundreds of dollars. [SECONDARY]
- A rival existed and won its own niche: DisplayPort, for PCs. It isn't free, but it's not tied to a studio-backed lock. [SECONDARY]
- A federal judge found the Adopter Agreement did not harm competition. [SECONDARY, PRIMARY quote of court's finding via HDMI LA/Constantine Cannon]
- **Why HDMI beat DisplayPort:** it arrived first (2002 vs May 2006) and was aimed at TVs, studios and set-top boxes, while DisplayPort was aimed at PC monitors. [SECONDARY, PCWorld and history summaries; say "arrived first" not "won by design"]
