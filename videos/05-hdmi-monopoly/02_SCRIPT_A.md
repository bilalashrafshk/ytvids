## ACT I — The plug is a contract; the mess of 2002

[WRY]
Tilt your head and look at the back of your TV. There's a row of ports. One of them is an HDMI port, a small flat plug with two clipped corners. You've pushed a cable into it a dozen times without a thought. [SHOWABLE: back of a TV, a hand pushing an HDMI cable into the port]

Before that TV left the factory, somebody paid to put that plug there. Not much. About four cents.

You never saw it on the receipt. It's folded into the price, like salt in bread. On a four hundred dollar TV, it's one dollar in every ten thousand. [SCENE: a shop receipt for a TV, the total circled, nothing else on it]

[BUILDING]
Now here's the part nobody mentions. That plug isn't a standard that belongs to everyone. It's a private contract, and it has been collecting money for twenty-three years. The money goes to a company called HDMI Licensing Administrator, which collects for the companies that built the plug. [DATA: counter, 2002 to today, "devices with the plug" ticking up]

This video follows one chain of cause and effect. Each link forces the next. By the end, you'll know who gets paid, and why the four cents is the smallest thing about it.

[WRY]
Start in 2002, with a mess.

Behind a TV of that year, you'd find a knot of leads. One for the picture. Two for the sound. Another if you wanted the picture sharper. Every one was a different plug, made by a different company, and they never quite fit. The back of a TV looked like a telephone switchboard. [REMOTION: ARCHETYPE_WHIP_ZOOM_MONTAGE — drawer of tangled cables, each lead in a different colour, whip between them]

Seven electronics companies were tired of it. Hitachi. Panasonic. Philips. Silicon Image. Sony. Thomson. Toshiba. They sold rival TVs, and they still sat down together. In April 2002, they started building one plug that carried picture and sound in a single cable. It even worked with the old computer plug, called DVI. [SHOWABLE: seven company badges at a long table]

Eight months later, on the ninth of December, it was done. They called it HDMI.

## ACT II — The studios, the lock and the loop

[BUILDING]
But a plug needed one more thing. Films.

Movies had gone digital, and a digital picture can be copied perfectly, again and again. The plan was to win over the film studios. So the plug carried a lock, supplied by Intel. Fox, Universal, Warner Bros. and Disney all backed it. So did the big satellite and cable companies. [SHOWABLE: the studio logos stacked beside the plug]

Here's how the lock works. Think of a badge. Every device that has the lock carries a set of secret keys. When your streaming box meets your TV, they check each other's badges. If the TV has no badge, the box won't send the film.

That is the whole trick. Not a fence around the plug, but a bouncer at the door.

[STILLNESS]
And the badges cost money. Making a device with the lock means paying a fee every year, fifteen thousand dollars, plus a price for each set of keys, which gets cheaper the more you buy.

[TENSE]
Can a lock like that be picked? Yes. And it was.

In September 2010, a master key turned up online. With it, anyone could make keys that looked real. Intel said it seemed to have been worked out from scratch, not leaked. Either way, it warned it would take legal action against anyone who built devices to get around the lock. [SCENE: a laptop screen, a string of characters, a padlock icon cracked open]

Five years on, a small box called the HDFury could strip the lock off a picture. Warner Bros. sued on the last day of 2015. The box's maker argued it only lowered the quality, which the law allows. They settled five months later.

The lock got picked. The chain didn't break.

[BUILDING]
Why not? Because by then, the chain had already moved.

Here is the whole loop, in four steps. One: studios and cable companies back the plug. Two: so the boxes people buy all use it. Three: so every TV has to have it. Four: so every TV maker signs the contract, and pays. More than two thousand one hundred companies have. [REMOTION: ARCHETYPE_3D_ORBITAL_FLYWHEEL — four numbered steps turning, the final step feeding back into the first]

[PIVOTAL]
Nobody voted for that. No law says every TV needs this plug. The market simply arrived at a place with one door.

Try building a TV without it. It would work. It just wouldn't plug into most of what you own.

## ACT III — The price

[WRY]
Picture a club with one entrance and a bouncer. Anyone can build a nicer door around the back. But the party is inside, and the party has the bouncer's badge.

So what does the club charge?

The contract is short on glamour. Every maker pays a yearly fee, ten thousand dollars for a big one. Small ones pay five thousand, plus a dollar a unit. On top comes the fee for each device, and this is the number in the title. As widely reported, it starts at fifteen cents. [SHOWABLE: a price sheet with three rows, the top row 15 cents]

Put the HDMI logo on your product and the fee drops to five cents. Add the lock as well and it drops to four.

Read that again. The toll gets cheaper if you advertise the toll company. The group's own logo rules say so, in writing: use the logo, get a discount. [SHOWABLE: the logo rules page, the words "discounted royalty rate" highlighted]

[STILLNESS]
Four cents. Less than a gumball.

[BUILDING]
But that's the price for the giants. Let's run it for a small maker. Say you build ten thousand TVs a year, in a garage that's turned into a factory. [SCENE: a small workshop, a row of unfinished TVs on a bench]

Your yearly fee is five thousand dollars. Then you pay a dollar on every TV, just to be in the system. Then the per-device fee, at five cents, comes to five hundred dollars. All told, about fifteen thousand five hundred dollars. That's a dollar fifty-five on every TV. [DATA: waterfall for one small maker, 5,000 + 10,000 + 500, ending on $1.55 per TV]

Now run it for a giant selling ten million TVs. The yearly fee is ten thousand dollars, and the per-device fee is four cents each. It comes to about four cents a TV.

So it isn't one price. The small maker pays nearly forty times what the giant pays. That's our own math on the reported fees. A cheap contract weighs the most on the people with the least.

[WRY]
And it's per device. A TV with five ports on the back pays once, not five times.

The fee comes with something else. A licence to the patents behind the plug. That's the part most people never notice. The contract doesn't just let you use the name. It lets you build the thing without being sued.

But only for a real HDMI product. Not a copy. Not a look-alike. The licence stops where the product stops.

So how much does a few cents add up to?

## ACT IV — The money, the rival and the court

[WRY]
In 2017 alone, the plug shipped in nearly nine hundred million devices. At four to fifteen cents each, that's tens of millions of dollars, in a single year. [DATA: 900 million devices × 4–15 cents, the answer a range in millions]

See how fast it grew. Six hundred million devices by early 2009. Two billion by late 2011. Nearly ten billion by 2021. [DATA: a staircase, 0.6 → 2 → 10 → 14 billion, one step lit at a time]

Today, close to fourteen billion. That's about two for every person on Earth. Multiply it out, and our own rough math says somewhere between six hundred million and two billion dollars, over twenty-three years. Most likely near the bottom of that.

That's real money. But it isn't Big Tech money. It doesn't need to be.

[BUILDING]
Because the money was never the point. The point is that nobody could say no.

Some tried. In 2006, a rival plug called DisplayPort arrived, built for computer screens. It came from a standards group, and it was sold as free. It never took over the living room. The TVs were already taken. [SHOWABLE: a PC monitor with DisplayPort next to a TV with HDMI]

And it isn't quite free. A group of patent owners asks twenty cents a product. That's more than the lowest fee on the HDMI side. Some of the names on that list are the same ones that built HDMI.

[WRY]
So the escape route has a toll too.

The group guards its name, too. It has taken makers to court for stamping the HDMI mark on products they hadn't signed for. Some were accused of doing it at a big electronics show, right in front of the buyers. [SCENE: a trade-show booth, a shelf of cables, a small HDMI logo on the packaging]

Then in 2011, the founders set up a second body, called the HDMI Forum. It writes the new versions of the plug. HDMI Licensing Administrator writes the contract. The Forum writes the rules. In June 2025, it released version two point two, and the same licensing company sells that one too. Both trace back to the same founders. The names on the door change. Thomson is called Vantiva now. The group doesn't change. [DATA: two boxes, "writes the rules" and "collects the fees", one arrow from the founders to each]

[BUILDING]
Which brings us to a chip company called Availink.

Availink made the little chips that go inside TVs. It sold them to other companies, who built the sets. So who owed the fee, the chip maker or the TV maker? The contract said the chip maker. Availink signed it, and it was licensed for about three years. Then the deal ended, and the group behind HDMI took it to court over unpaid fees.

Availink hit back. It argued that the group was breaking competition law, and that the contract kept rivals out. [SHOWABLE: a court docket page, the case name highlighted]

On the last day of 2025, a federal judge ruled for the group, and threw out every one of Availink's claims. Call it a monopoly or don't. The judge found the contract had not hurt competition. And the contract really did require the chip company to pay fees on its chips.

[STILLNESS]
A week before the trial, Availink agreed to pay fourteen million dollars. Then it signed the contract again.

[BUILDING]
Think about what that means. A company whose whole business was the chips inside TVs couldn't beat a contract that costs cents. The cheap fee was never the wall. The wall was everything around it: the patents, the name, the lock, and a court that upheld the contract.

## ACT V — The gate and the close

[WRY]
Let's be fair. There's a real case for all this. One plug is why your console works with any TV, in any country. That takes testing, and it takes someone keeping fakes off the shelf. That's the best argument for it. And when a plug does its job, nobody thinks about it at all.

And a judge looked at the contract and found no harm.

But hold on.

[BUILDING]
The contract is about money. The Forum is about who gets to see the plans.

In 2021, the Forum closed the newest version of the plug's rulebook to the public. Then AMD, one of the biggest makers of graphics chips, tried to build an open-source driver for it on Linux. That's free code that anyone can read. [DATA: a timeline bar, 2023 to 2024, "AMD's lawyers work", "decision with the Forum"]

They worked on it for months. In early 2024, an AMD engineer wrote: "The HDMI Forum has rejected our proposal unfortunately. At this time an open source HDMI two point one implementation is not possible without running afoul of the HDMI Forum requirements." [SHOWABLE: the AMD engineer's message, the rejection line highlighted]

So on Linux, with an AMD card, the best picture over HDMI is off the table. No four-K at a hundred and twenty frames a second. AMD's advice to gamers? Use DisplayPort.

[WRY]
And here's a small joke on top. Since 2023, a port can be sold as "HDMI two point one" and only do what the old one did. The group's answer, as reported: the old version no longer exists. The only way to tell is the small print on the spec sheet. [SCENE: a TV spec sheet, "HDMI 2.1" printed beside a port that does less]

[STILLNESS]
Go back to that TV. The plug with two clipped corners. It looked like a piece of the world, like a wall socket. It was a piece of a contract.

Nobody ever voted on the plug behind your TV. Nobody has ever seen the bill.

[BUILDING]
The question isn't what it costs. It's who else has a lock on something you never noticed you were using. [REMOTION: ARCHETYPE_INFINITE_PORTAL_TUNNEL — zoom out from the TV port to a wall of ports on every device in a house]
