## ACT I — The paradox and layer one: the fee

[TENSE]
On the last day of 2025, a federal judge threw out every claim a chip company had made against the plug on the back of your TV. A week before the trial, that company agreed to pay fourteen million dollars, and then it signed a new contract. [SHOWABLE: a court docket page, the case name and the date highlighted]

What it fought over was four cents.

[WRY]
That should make no sense. A fee that small is not worth a lawyer's phone call, let alone a trial. And a plug this ordinary, one you've pushed a cable into a hundred times without looking, feels like the sort of thing nobody could own. You've never been asked to agree to anything. You've never seen a price. It comes with the TV, like the screws in the back, and it seems to belong to the world the way a wall socket does. [SHOWABLE: the back of a TV, a hand pushing an HDMI cable into the port]

So how did a company that knew the plug better than almost anyone end up losing, paying, and signing up again? The answer sits in three layers, and each one is bigger than the last. [REMOTION: ARCHETYPE_WHIP_ZOOM_MONTAGE — a drawer of tangled cables, each lead whipping past to the same small flat plug]

[BUILDING]
Start with the smallest layer, the fee itself.

Before that TV left the factory, somebody paid to put the plug there. You never saw it on the receipt. On a four hundred dollar TV, four cents is one dollar in every ten thousand, folded into the price like salt in bread.

HDMI is a private contract. Any company that wants to make a device with that plug signs it, pays a yearly fee, and pays again on every device it sells. As widely reported, the per-device fee starts at fifteen cents. Put the HDMI logo on the product and it falls to five. Add the copy-protection lock as well and it falls to four. [SHOWABLE: a price sheet with three rows, fifteen cents, five cents, four cents]

Read that again, because it's the odd part. The group's own rules say a maker gets a cheaper rate for advertising the group. The toll goes down the more you promote the toll company. [SHOWABLE: the logo rules page, the words "discounted royalty rate" highlighted]

And here's the twist inside the smallest layer. Four cents isn't what everyone pays. It's what the giants pay.

## ACT II — The giants and the small makers; layer two: the lock

[STILLNESS]
Say you build ten thousand TVs a year in a workshop that used to be a garage. Your yearly fee is five thousand dollars. On top of that, every TV costs you a dollar just to be in the system, and five more cents for the plug itself. It comes to about fifteen thousand five hundred dollars, a dollar fifty-five on every TV. [DATA: waterfall for one small maker, 5,000 + 10,000 + 500, ending on $1.55 per TV]

A giant selling ten million TVs pays ten thousand a year and four cents a set. About four cents a TV, all in.

[BUILDING]
So the small maker pays nearly forty times what the giant pays. That's our own math on the reported fees. The fee that looks tiny is only tiny if you're already huge. [REMOTION: ARCHETYPE_SPLIT_SCREEN_COMPARISON — a workshop bench beside a factory floor, the cost per TV shown on each side]

Watch how fast it spread. Six hundred million devices by early 2009. Two billion by late 2011, with eleven hundred companies signed up. Nearly ten billion by 2021. [DATA: a staircase, 0.6 → 2 → 10 → 14 billion, one step lit at a time]

Today it's nearly fourteen billion, about two for every person on Earth. Across the whole world, that still isn't a huge pile. Multiply it out, and our own rough math puts the total somewhere between six hundred million and two billion dollars over twenty-three years. Real money, but nothing like Big Tech.

So the fee alone can't explain why a company would lose and pay and sign again. Something else is holding the door shut.

[TENSE]
That something is the second layer, and it's the lock.

Back in 2002, seven electronics companies built HDMI together: Hitachi, Panasonic, Philips, Silicon Image, Sony, Thomson and Toshiba. They were rivals who sold competing TVs. Their aim was one plug that carried picture and sound in a single cable, replacing a knot of leads that never quite fit. It even worked with the old computer plug, called DVI. [SHOWABLE: seven company badges at a long table]

But movies had gone digital, and a digital picture can be copied perfectly. The plan was to win over the film studios. So the plug carried a lock, supplied by Intel. Fox, Universal, Warner Bros. and Disney backed it, and so did the big satellite and cable companies.

The lock works like a badge. Every device that has it carries secret keys. When your streaming box meets your TV, they check each other's badges. If the TV has none, the box refuses to send the film. [SCENE: a streaming box and a TV, a badge passing between them, then a second TV with no badge and a black screen]

[STILLNESS]
Now think about what that means for a TV maker. You could build a TV without the plug. It would work perfectly well. It just wouldn't take a film from any box, console or player you own.

That's the physical stake. What disappears if you skip the contract isn't a discount. It's the ability to sell a TV that plugs into anything.

## ACT III — The lock is picked; layer three: the patents

[BUILDING]
Can the lock be picked? It was. In September 2010, a master key turned up online, and with it anyone could make keys that looked real. Intel said it seemed to have been worked out from scratch, not leaked, and it warned it would take legal action against anyone who built devices to get around the lock. [SCENE: a laptop screen, a string of characters, a padlock icon cracked open]

Five years later, a small box called the HDFury could strip the lock from a picture. Warner Bros. sued on the last day of 2015. The box's maker said it only lowered the quality, which the law allows, and the two sides settled five months later.

Even so, the plug stayed everywhere. The lock got picked. The chain of makers, boxes and studios had already locked itself in. Every box had the plug, so every TV needed it, so every TV maker signed.

[PIVOTAL]
That is why the fee can be so small. It doesn't have to be big to work. Everything around it is doing the holding.

[TENSE]
Now the third layer, the biggest one. The fee gets you a plug. It also gets you something most people never hear about, a licence to the patents behind it.

The contract doesn't only let a maker use the name. It lets a maker build the thing without being sued. But only for a real HDMI product, not a copy, and the licence stops where the product stops.

A chip maker is the sharpest case, and this one was called Availink. It sells chips to other companies, not to you. So when its chips end up inside finished TVs, who pays the fee? That was the fight. The group said the chip company owed it. The chip company said the whole arrangement was illegal, that the group and its founders held so much power over the plug that the contract kept rivals out. [SHOWABLE: a court docket page, the counterclaim highlighted]

On every count, the judge disagreed. Nothing in the contract had hurt competition, and the chip company had not been harmed by it. And the contract really did require the chip company to pay fees on its chips. It had been licensed for about three years before the deal ended.

A week before trial, it paid fourteen million dollars. Then it signed the contract again, and joined more than two thousand one hundred other companies on the list.

[STILLNESS]
That isn't a company that lost an argument about four cents. It's a company that found out what the plug is actually attached to.

## ACT IV — The nested paradox and the gate

[WRY]
Now here's a second puzzle, and it sits inside the first.

If the plug comes with all this control, why doesn't everyone just use the other one? There is another one. DisplayPort arrived in 2006, built for computer screens by the people who make computer monitors. It came from a standards group, and it was sold as free, faster and open to everyone. If HDMI is a toll, why not take the free road?

Two reasons. First, timing. By 2006, HDMI was four years old and already on the TVs, boxes and consoles in living rooms. DisplayPort never took over the living room. Second, it isn't quite free. A group of patent owners asks twenty cents a product, which is more than the lowest fee on the HDMI side. Some of the names on that patent list are the same companies that built HDMI in the first place. [SHOWABLE: a PC monitor with DisplayPort beside a TV with HDMI, a price tag of twenty cents on the DisplayPort]

So the exit has a toll too. That's the nested paradox. The escape route isn't a way out of the system, only a different door in the same building.

[TENSE]
There's one more piece, and it's where the third layer bites hardest.

The company that collects the fee doesn't even own the plug. It's an agent, appointed by the founders to license it. In 2011, the founders set up a second body, called the HDMI Forum. The first company writes the contract. The Forum writes the rules for each new version of the plug. In 2021, the Forum closed the newest version of the rulebook to the public. [DATA: two boxes, "writes the rules" and "collects the fees", one arrow from the founders to each]

Then AMD, one of the biggest makers of graphics chips, tried to build an open-source driver for that version on Linux. That's free code that anyone can read. It worked on it for months, alongside its own lawyers. In early 2024, an AMD engineer wrote: "The HDMI Forum has rejected our proposal unfortunately. At this time an open source HDMI two point one implementation is not possible without running afoul of the HDMI Forum requirements." [SHOWABLE: the AMD engineer's message, the rejection line highlighted]

The cost is physical. On Linux, with an AMD card, the best picture over HDMI is off the table. No four-K at a hundred and twenty frames a second. AMD's advice to gamers was to use DisplayPort. It's a small group of people. But it's the first time the plans themselves became the price.

[BUILDING]
And to top it off, since 2023, a port can be sold as "HDMI two point one" while doing only what the older version did. The group's answer, as reported: the old version no longer exists. [SCENE: a TV spec sheet, "HDMI 2.1" printed beside a port that does less]

## ACT V — The honest case and the button

[WRY]
Let's be fair to the other side. One plug is why your console works with any TV, in any country. That takes testing, and it takes someone keeping fakes off the shelf. A judge looked at the contract and found it did no harm to competition. It's a real argument.

The names on the door change. Thomson is called Vantiva now. The group doesn't change. And it guards its name. It has taken makers to court for stamping the HDMI mark on products they hadn't signed for, and some were accused of doing it at a big electronics show, right in front of the buyers.

[STILLNESS]
So return to the very first question. Why would a company fight over four cents, lose, pay, and sign up again?

Because the four cents was never what it was buying. It was buying a way into every living room, and the price of staying out was everything else. A TV that can't take a film from your box isn't a cheaper TV. It's a TV nobody can use.

[BUILDING]
Your instinct says small means harmless, and free means nobody's charging. Three things say otherwise. A fee that shrinks the bigger you are. A lock that makes the plug the only way in. And a rulebook that can close the door on anyone who can't sign.

Go back to the back of your TV. The plug with two clipped corners. It looked like a piece of the world. It was a piece of a contract.

Nobody ever voted on the plug behind your TV. Nobody has ever seen the bill. And the cheapest-looking thing in your living room turns out to be the one somebody thought hardest about.
