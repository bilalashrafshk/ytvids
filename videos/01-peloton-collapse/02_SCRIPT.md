# 02_SCRIPT: The $50 Billion Fitness Mirage

### Narrative Structure & Technical Parameters
- **Chosen Structure**: **In Medias Res / Forensic Investigation Frame**
- **Rationale**: Starting at the physical monument of the collapse—an abandoned, half-built 1.2-million-square-foot factory in Ohio sold for pennies on the dollar—immediately grounds a complex financial breakdown in a tangible, inescapable reality before peeling back the accounting illusion.
- **Estimated Runtime**: ~14 minutes (~2,150 words at ~155 words/minute).
- **Format**: Documented Case (Illustrated caricatures for corporate executives, clean bordered insets for primary filings, anonymized silhouettes for injured users and consumers).

---

## The Script

### Act I: The Steel Skeleton in Ohio (0:00 – 2:30)

In January 2024, a solar panel manufacturer named First Solar quietly finalized a real estate purchase in Wood County, Ohio.

`[SHOWABLE: Wood County Deed Record — First Solar Purchase of Peloton Output Park for $33M]`

The purchase price was thirty-three million dollars.

For that money, they didn't just get two hundred acres of prime industrial farmland. They got a 1 point 2 million square foot unfinished monolith of structural steel, poured concrete, and vacant assembly bays. 

Thirty-three million sounds like real money. Until you look at the company that poured the concrete.

`[TENSE]` Less than three years earlier, this exact parcel of dirt was announced as the crown jewel of American consumer tech. It had a name: the Peloton Output Park. A four-hundred-million-dollar mega-factory engineered to stamp out 1 point 5 million connected exercise bikes every single year, employing two thousand American workers.

`[DATA: $400M Planned Investment vs. $33M Liquidation Price]`

Four hundred million dollars committed. Thirty-three million recovered. A seventy-plus percent loss on invested capital, wiped out before a single stationary bike ever rolled off the line.

`[STILLNESS]` Most people remember the Peloton story as a comedy of errors. They remember the viral 2019 Christmas commercial where an anxious wife filmed a video diary about her exercise bike. 

`[WRY]` They remember that episode of the *Sex and the City* reboot where Mr. Big suffered a fatal heart attack on a Peloton Bike Plus, tanking the company’s stock eleven percent overnight. And they remember Ryan Reynolds frantically producing a parody commercial forty-eight hours later with the actor to prove he was still alive—only for the campaign to implode four days later when the actor was hit with serious sexual assault allegations.

It was chaotic, it was tabloid-ready, and it made for great memes.

`[TENSE]` But here is the reality: Mr. Big didn't kill Peloton. Ryan Reynolds didn't kill Peloton. And the viral Christmas ad didn't erase fifty billion dollars of enterprise value.

`[SHOWABLE: SEC Form 10-K FY 2022 — Consolidated Statements of Operations]`

What destroyed Peloton was a lethal accounting mirage. Corporate executives convinced Wall Street—and themselves—that a capital-heavy manufacturer of heavy consumer metal was actually an asset-light Silicon Valley software platform. 

And by the time they realized that every single bike rolling out of their warehouses was actively bleeding hundreds of dollars of cash, they had already spent billions building an industrial empire for customers that did not exist.

`[BUILDING]` This is the forensic autopsy of how fifty billion dollars vanished into thin air.

---

### Act II: The SaaS Illusion (2:30 – 5:30)

`[CARICATURE: John Foley]`
To understand how the machine broke, you have to look at the man who built it: John Foley. 

Foley was not an industrial supply chain engineer. He was an e-commerce executive who spent years pitching venture capitalists on a simple, intoxicating premise.

`[WRY]` He didn't want to sell you a metal exercise bike. He wanted to sell you an ongoing lifestyle subscription.

When Peloton went public on the NASDAQ in September 2019, institutional investors didn't value it like NordicTrack or Nautilus. Those traditional fitness companies traded at boring, cyclical multiples—maybe one or two times their annual revenue.

Peloton claimed to be fundamentally different. In their investor pitch, the exercise bike was just a trojan horse. You buy the hardware once for twenty-two hundred dollars, and then you pay thirty-nine dollars every single month, forever, to stream live workouts from charismatic celebrity instructors.

On paper, the unit economics were gorgeous. 

`[DATA: FY 2020 Hardware Margin — $2,150 ASP vs. +43.1% Gross Margin]`

In software, Customer Acquisition Cost—or CAC—is the money you spend on marketing to win one paying customer. Usually, that marketing expense burns cash upfront. 

`[SHOWABLE: SEC Form 10-K FY 2020 — Segment Reporting Note]`

But throughout fiscal 2020, Peloton had a secret weapon. Because affluent early adopters were happy to pay over two thousand dollars for the bike, Peloton made roughly forty-three percent gross profit on the physical hardware alone. 

That meant every time someone bought a bike, Peloton cleared roughly nine hundred dollars of gross cash before the customer even turned on the screen. 

That nine hundred dollars completely paid for the Facebook ads, the showroom leases in upscale shopping malls, and the marketing campaigns. In Wall Street terms, their customer acquisition was virtually free. And once that customer was plugged in, they stayed. Monthly churn was under one percent. 

`[BUILDING]` Wall Street looked at those numbers and lost its mind. Investors said: *This isn't a hardware business. This is Netflix on a bike.*

`[TENSE]` And then came March 2020.

COVID-19 shuttered every commercial gym in North America and Europe overnight. Millions of professionals with disposable income found themselves trapped in their living rooms, terrified and bored.

The demand didn't just climb; it detonated. 

Monthly workouts per subscriber surged past twenty-four. Delivery backlogs stretched from two weeks to eight weeks, then to ten weeks. Customer service phone lines were overwhelmed. In December 2020, Peloton’s market valuation passed fifty billion dollars. John Foley was a billionaire. The instructors were signing seven-figure talent contracts.

`[PIVOTAL]` But right here—at the absolute peak of the frenzy—executive leadership made the foundational mistake that would seal their fate.

They confused a temporary, crisis-driven pull-forward of demand with a permanent, structural expansion of their market.

They looked at customers frantically ordering bikes during a global quarantine and assumed that this was the new normal baseline of human civilization. 

And instead of staying asset-light and letting customers wait out the pandemic, Peloton decided to build an industrial empire.

### Act III: The Bullwhip Catastrophe (5:30 – 8:30)

`[TENSE]` In logistics, there is a famous concept known as the "bullwhip effect." 

Imagine you are holding a whip. If you flick your wrist just two inches, the tip of the whip fifty feet away cracks violently through the air across twenty feet. 

In industrial manufacturing, customer demand is the wrist. If consumer demand temporarily jumps twenty percent, retailers order forty percent more to avoid stocking out. Wholesalers see that and order eighty percent more. And the factory at the end of the chain ramps up capacity by two hundred percent.

Peloton's executive suite took the bullwhip and swung it with both hands.

`[SHOWABLE: Precor Acquisition Filing — December 2020 ($420M Cash)]`

In December 2020, Peloton paid four hundred and twenty million dollars in pure cash to buy Precor, an established commercial fitness equipment manufacturer. The goal? Grab six hundred thousand square feet of factory space in North Carolina and Washington to build bikes domestically.

`[WRY]` Think about the irony: they bought a company that made equipment for commercial gyms, at the exact moment commercial gyms were closed, hoping to convert the factories to make home bikes.

`[DATA: $100M Incremental Air Freight Expense — Q2/Q3 FY 2021]`

Next came the freight panic. Port congestion in Long Beach and Los Angeles was delaying shipping containers coming from Taiwan. Rather than letting customers wait, Peloton began chartering Boeing 747 cargo planes. They literally loaded 135-pound steel exercise bikes into airplanes and flew them across the Pacific Ocean, burning roughly one hundred million dollars in incremental air cargo fees just to shave three weeks off delivery times.

And then, in May 2021, came the big bet: the four-hundred-million-dollar Peloton Output Park in Troy Township, Ohio. 

`[MAP: Troy Township, Wood County, Ohio]`

Two thousand workers. 1 point 2 million square feet. Foley declared that Peloton would control its own destiny, manufacturing its own products on American soil.

Structural steel began rising from the Ohio dirt in late 2021.

`[STILLNESS]` At that exact moment, the real world reopened.

Vaccines rolled out. People walked out of their apartments. Commercial gyms unlocked their doors. 

And the demand for twenty-two-hundred-dollar stationary bikes didn't just normalize. It fell off a cliff.

`[TENSE]` An exercise bike is not an iPhone. It does not break every three years, and there is no software update that forces you to buy a new frame. It is an eight-year consumer durable good made of welded steel and flywheels. 

Every wealthy household that wanted a Peloton already had one sitting in their spare bedroom. 

The backlog evaporated overnight. But the container ships full of raw parts were still crossing the ocean. The Asian contract manufacturers still had legally binding purchase orders. And in Ohio, workers were still pouring concrete for a factory designed to pump out 1 point 5 million bikes a year for people who were suddenly running outside.

The bullwhip snapped back. And the crack shattered their balance sheet.

### Act IV: The Inverted Math & The Safety Disaster (8:30 – 11:30)

`[SHOWABLE: SEC Form 10-K FY 2022 — Hardware Gross Margin Breakdown]`

Look closely at Peloton’s Form 10-K filing for the fiscal year ended June 30, 2022. This is where the accounting mirage dissolves.

To clear mountains of bikes piling up in third-party warehouses, management started slashing prices. They dropped the entry-level bike from over two thousand dollars down to fourteen hundred dollars. 

Meanwhile, ocean container rates had spiked five-fold, storage fees were compounding daily, and air cargo bills were coming due.

`[DATA: Unit Economics Collapse — From +$927 Profit to -$196 Loss Per Unit]`

In fiscal 2020, every bike delivered generated nine hundred and twenty-seven dollars in gross profit. 

By fiscal 2022, every single bike delivered generated a loss of one hundred and ninety-six dollars. 

`[TENSE]` Understand what that means: the SaaS formula had inverted. 

Previously, hardware gross profit paid for customer acquisition. Now, acquiring a customer meant paying for marketing *and* taking a direct two-hundred-dollar cash loss the second the delivery van pulled into their driveway. In the final quarter of fiscal 2022, hardware gross margins hit negative ninety-eight percent.

`[SOMBER]` And while the balance sheet was bleeding out, a devastating engineering defect became public.

Peloton had launched a luxury motorized treadmill: the Tread Plus, priced at forty-three hundred dollars. 

Unlike traditional treadmills that use a flat continuous belt sliding across a deck with an inch of clearance, the Tread Plus used fifty-nine thick rubberized aluminum slats driven on a motorized track. 

Crucially, the elevated rear roller sat six to eight inches above the floor without a physical safety guard. The motorized roller created an aggressive pinch hazard. 

In early 2021, horrific incident reports began piling up on the public docket of the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission. Exercise balls, family pets, and small children were being pulled underneath the operating machine. In March 2021, a six-year-old child died from compressive asphyxiation.

`[SHOWABLE: CPSC Urgent Administrative Public Health Alert — April 17, 2021]`

On April 17, 2021, the CPSC issued an extraordinary public warning urging consumers with children and pets to stop using the Tread Plus immediately.

What did Peloton’s executive leadership do? 

`[TENSE]` Instead of an immediate pause and voluntary recall, Peloton released an aggressive corporate statement attacking the federal agency, calling the CPSC’s warning "inaccurate and misleading." 

It was a staggering display of corporate hubris. For eighteen days, management dug in while national news broadcasts aired viral home security footage of toddlers being pulled under the machines. 

`[SOMBER]` On May 5, John Foley was forced to make a humiliating public apology. The company announced a full voluntary recall of 125,000 Tread Plus units, halting sales indefinitely and recording an immediate twenty-six million dollar direct revenue hit.

`[SHOWABLE: CPSC Settlement Record — $19.065M Civil Penalty (January 2023)]`

Eighteen months later, federal regulators would extract their pound of flesh: a nineteen-million-dollar civil penalty. 

The government’s consent order revealed that Peloton had received more than one hundred and fifty reports of people and objects being pulled beneath the machine—including seventy-two documented injuries—before it ever formally notified regulators. 

Even worse, the audit found that Peloton had knowingly delivered thirty-eight recalled Tread Plus machines to customers *after* the official recall had already been enacted.

The brand's premium halo—the trust of affluent suburban parents—was broken beyond repair.

### Act V: The Activist Strike & The Reckoning (11:30 – 14:00)

`[TENSE]` By January 2022, the company was hemorrhaging cash at a rate of over two hundred million dollars every single month.

Why hadn't the board of directors stopped the bleeding months earlier?

`[SHOWABLE: SEC Form 10-K — Dual-Class Share Ownership Table]`

Because of a corporate voting moat. When Peloton went public, it issued two classes of stock. Class A public shares carried one vote. But Class B shares, held by John Foley and his founding inner circle, carried twenty votes per share. 

With less than twenty percent of the actual economic ownership, Foley and his team controlled over eighty percent of the voting power. Outside public shareholders could do nothing while capex soared, Manhattan office leases were signed, and private executive jets were chartered.

`[SHOWABLE: Blackwells Capital 65-Page Presentation — January 2022]`

The wall finally broke on January 24, 2022. Activist investment fund Blackwells Capital published a blistering sixty-five-page presentation demanding the immediate termination of John Foley and the outright sale of the company. 

Blackwells laid out the catastrophic forensic record: selling, general, and administrative expenses had ballooned while revenue was falling. Inventory write-downs were topping three hundred million dollars. The company had spent nearly a billion dollars on factory capacity it would never use.

Foley tried to hold on. But institutional lenders and Class A shareholders delivered an ultimatum.

`[CARICATURE: Barry McCarthy]`
On February 8, 2022, John Foley was removed as CEO. In his place, the board installed Barry McCarthy—the former CFO of Netflix and Spotify. 

McCarthy was an institutional surgeon. He had zero emotional attachment to Foley's dream of an American manufacturing empire.

Within forty-eight hours, McCarthy swung the axe:
- Construction on the $400M Ohio mega-factory was permanently stopped.
- Two thousand eight hundred jobs were eliminated in a single day.
- Proprietary delivery vans were shut down, replaced with third-party delivery contractors.
- Proprietary factories were abandoned; all hardware manufacturing was surrendered back to contract partners in Taiwan.

`[DATA: FY 2022 Losses — $2.83 Billion Net Loss, -$2.46 Billion Free Cash Flow]`

When the final accounting books closed on fiscal 2022, the damage was staggering: a net loss of 2.83 billion dollars, negative 2.46 billion dollars of free cash flow, and 337 million dollars of obsolete inventory written directly to zero.

McCarthy spent two years ruthlessly cutting costs, shrinking headcount from over nine thousand down to three thousand, raising seven hundred and fifty million dollars in emergency debt, and narrowing cash burn down to negative one hundred and twenty-five million. 

`[WRY]` But cutting costs does not create new demand. 

By 2024, Peloton faced a new structural nightmare: their own past success. 

Millions of pristine, barely-used Peloton bikes were sitting in basements. Hundreds of thousands were listed on Facebook Marketplace, eBay, and Craigslist for four hundred dollars—a seventy-five percent discount to retail. 

`[SHOWABLE: Peloton Support Notice — August 2024 $95 Used Equipment Activation Fee]`

Every time someone bought a second-hand bike on Craigslist, Peloton made zero dollars in hardware revenue, but still had to maintain server infrastructure, stream content, and provide customer support. 

In August 2024, in an act of pure desperation, Peloton instituted a mandatory ninety-five-dollar "Used Equipment Activation Fee" just to turn on a second-hand bike. It was the ultimate confession: they were no longer a hyper-growth tech platform; they were a utility company trying to tax its own aging machines.

`[CARICATURE: Barry McCarthy]`
On May 2, 2024, exhausted and out of levers to pull, Barry McCarthy resigned.

`[STILLNESS]` On January 1, 2025, Peter Stern—the co-founder of Apple Fitness Plus—takes over as CEO of Peloton. 

He inherits a business valued not at fifty billion dollars, but at roughly two billion. A business that must survive not by manufacturing dreams of limitless growth, but by milking subscription fees from an installed base of stationary bikes that are slowly gathering dust in American living rooms.

`[TENSE]` The lesson of the Peloton collapse is not about exercise, or marketing, or viral TV episodes. 

It is the oldest lesson in corporate finance: you cannot value a factory like an algorithm. 

`[SHOWABLE: Satellite Image / Clean Inset of First Solar Ohio Facility]`

When a once-in-a-century crisis pulls tomorrow's demand into today, the discipline lies in knowing that tomorrow will still come. 

And if you spend billions building an empire for a world that only exists inside a quarantine, the bill will always come due.

Just ask the solar company that bought a four-hundred-million-dollar monument to hubris in Ohio... for thirty-three million dollars.
