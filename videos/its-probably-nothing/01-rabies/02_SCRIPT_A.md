Dennis Fine is forty-three, and his left forearm has been tingling since Tuesday. It isn't painful. It's more like a foot waking up, except it's an arm, and it isn't finishing the job. [SCENE: a mid-century kitchen, morning; Dennis rubbing his forearm; mug reading "World's Okayest Grillmaster"] [BUBBLE: "It's probably nothing."]

He also has a low fever, a headache, and a tiredness that the second coffee hasn't touched. Dennis, being a man of science, has four theories. He slept on it wrong. It's the new laundry detergent. Work has been stressful. And something is definitely going round the office.

He types "tingling arm fever" into his phone. The first result is a forum, and the top comment is from a man named Rick, who is certain it's his elbow. Dennis stops reading there. This is what he calls research. [SCENE: phone screen, the first result, Dennis's thumb already moving away]

[SETUP: Gary's cousin] Then he texts Gary from accounts, whose cousin once had the same thing. Gary says it went away on its own. Dennis feels much better. It does not occur to him to ask what the cousin's thing actually was.

So he decides to wait it out. It'll be gone by the weekend. He's got a barbecue on Saturday. [STILLNESS]

In three American cases in twenty twenty-one, death came about two to three weeks after the first symptom. Dennis has one week of plans. And the only decision that could have changed any of this was made six weeks ago, at five in the morning, by a man holding a towel.

[SCENE: hard cut to full colour, the colour layers perfectly in register. A wooden cabin at dawn. Caption: "Six weeks earlier."]

Same man. Same mug. A rented fishing cabin, five o'clock, and something is moving around the ceiling.

Dennis wakes up, sees a small dark shape doing laps, and reacts the way a brave man does. He pulls the blanket over his head. Then he throws a towel at it. The bat finds the open window and leaves. [SCENE: a bat circling; Dennis half under a blanket; a towel in mid-air]

Dennis sits up and does what everybody does. He checks himself. No blood. No marks. Nothing hurts. By breakfast it's a funny story, and the whole table laughs. [BUBBLE: "It's probably nothing."]

But before the laughing stops, three beliefs settle into Dennis's head. He doesn't say them out loud. He doesn't have to.

One: I'd know if it bit me. Two: if it were serious, I'd feel something soon. Three: if it ever got bad, a doctor could fix it. [PLATE: three printed index cards, one for each belief]

All three are wrong. And each is a little more wrong than the one before. [WRY]

But first, back to the cabin, because this is where the story splits. [SCENE: split screen; left "Ignoring-Dennis", right "Goes-on-Day-3 Dennis"; the same cabin, the same towel]

On the left, Dennis finishes his fishing weekend and drives home. The bat joins his collection of funny stories. By the following week, he's forgotten it entirely.

On the right, a different Dennis. Same cabin, same towel, same breakfast. But on the third day, a small voice asks him, did that thing touch me? So he phones a clinic. And, being Dennis, he apologises. He says sorry for bothering them. He says it's probably nothing. The nurse says, "That's what we're here for." [BUBBLE: "I'm so sorry to bother you."]

For the rest of this, we'll follow the Dennis on the left. We'll check in on the one on the right when it matters.

[BUILDING]
Belief one. I'd know if it bit me.

It's a reasonable belief. A bite hurts. A bite bleeds. You'd see it. But a bat's teeth are tiny, and the wounds they leave might not be easy to see at all. Whatever else you say about bats, they leave no evidence. [SCENE: white plate; an enlarged bat skull with tiny teeth; a ruler beside it]

Of the twenty-one bat-linked rabies cases in the United States between 1980 and 1999, twelve involved people who had touched or been near a bat but had no bite anyone could find. [DATA: 21 dots, 12 highlighted]

[CASE: Minnesota 2024] Real people have lived this. In twenty twenty-four, a woman in Minnesota found a bat in her sink. She killed it with a hammer, and threw it away. Nobody tested the bat. [SCENE: a sink, a hammer on the counter, the colour layers slipping very slightly]

Dennis's bat was in the air, not in a sink. But the rule is the same. Waking up in a room with a bat counts as a possible exposure, even if you can't find a mark. A doctor, sometimes with a test on the bat, decides whether treatment is needed.

It's why the numbers look the way they do. Of the forty-two people who caught rabies inside the United States between two thousand and twenty twenty-four, thirty-five got it from bats.

So belief one fails. Dennis would not have known. Neither would the other Dennis. The difference is that he asked. [SCENE: the right-hand Dennis in a clinic chair, relaxed]

[WRY]
Belief two. If it were serious, I'd feel something soon.

This is the one that does the real damage, because it's the one that feels like common sense. Serious things announce themselves. Rabies doesn't.

First, what a virus actually is. A virus is a small piece of genetic code in a protein coat. It cannot make copies of itself on its own. It has to get inside a cell and use the cell's own machinery to do it. Think of a recipe with no kitchen. Dennis, as it happens, is very well stocked with kitchens. [SCENE: white plate; a recipe card in a navy coat drifting towards a row of tiny kitchens]

Rabies lives in saliva. If that saliva reaches a bite, a scratch, or a damaged bit of skin, the virus gets in. [SCENE: white plate; a small bite shape on a forearm, a single clinical-red dot]

And then, for most of what follows, almost nothing happens. The virus is still at or near the site of the bite for most of the incubation period. Think of a visitor who arrives, sits down in the hallway, and keeps very quiet. Nothing is broken. Nobody has noticed. It is, in its way, the politest houseguest in medicine: no noise, no mess, no questions. The longest part of rabies isn't a journey. It's a wait.

How long? Typically two to three months. But as short as one week, and as long as a year. [SCENE: a calendar, pages turning, no marks on it]

At some point, the virus finds a nerve ending. And here's where it gets clever. Nerve cells already run a delivery system, a kind of internal railway that carries cargo from the fingertip back towards the cell's centre. The virus doesn't break in. It catches a lift. Free ride, one destination, and the destination is Dennis. [DATA: a teal nerve cable; the red dot steps aboard a small cart]

It rides the nerve towards the brain, carried by the cell's own machinery. And it doesn't use the blood. There is still no credible evidence that the blood plays a significant role in getting it to the brain. [DATA: the cart climbing the cable, slowly; the colour layers drifting out of register]

Which is why nothing rings. No flood in the bloodstream. No alarm. For weeks, sometimes months, nothing happens at all.

Dennis, meanwhile, is having a wonderful time. The bat story is a hit. He tells it to Gary. He tells it to his barber. He tells it at the barbecue, where the bat is now the size of a pigeon, and by week five, a small dog. Everybody laughs. He has told that story eleven times, to eleven people, and not one of them is a doctor. [SCENE: a barbecue; a speech bubble above Dennis with a bat that gets bigger each time; Dennis in full colour, the colour layers a hair out of register]

[CASE: Wisconsin 2004] Here's what that looks like. Wisconsin, two thousand four. A fifteen-year-old girl is bitten on the finger by a bat at church. The wound is about five millimetres long. Someone cleans it with hydrogen peroxide. She gets no rabies treatment. About a month later, her hand starts to tingle. [SCENE: a church hall, a girl holding a finger, a bat shape on the rafters]

Meanwhile, on the right, Dennis has a sore arm and a few appointments. He thanks the nurse four times. He apologises to the needle. The treatment starts with washing the wound. Then a shot of ready-made antibodies, which gives his body a head start, and a series of four vaccine doses over two weeks. That's the whole price. [SCENE: right-hand Dennis, sleeve rolled, a plaster, checking his watch]

It works by stopping the virus from reaching the central nervous system. Treatment after an exposure is nearly a hundred percent effective, if it's given before symptoms. CDC's own guidelines say no failures have been documented in the United States since today's vaccines were licensed. In medicine, that is about as close as it gets to a standing ovation.

So belief two fails the cruellest way. The quiet isn't the sign that nothing is wrong. The quiet is the only time anything works. [STILLNESS]

[BUILDING]
Belief three. If it ever got bad, a doctor could fix it.

This is week six. We're back in the kitchen. A quick note: Dennis's timeline is illustrative, and real patients vary widely. The oldest, quietest wound in the story is suddenly the loudest thing in the room. Tingling, itching, or pain at the old bite, with a flu-like illness. [SCENE: the kitchen again; Dennis's forearm, the colour layers visibly out of register]

On the right, the same morning, Dennis is flipping burgers. His arm feels exactly like an arm. [SCENE: right-hand Dennis at a barbecue, full colour, in register]

Dennis does the sensible thing. He goes to see a doctor. He sits in the waiting room for forty minutes, reading about boats and rehearsing his apology. The doctor asks about the arm, the fever, the headache. She asks if he's been near any animals. And Dennis thinks about the bat. Briefly. But that's a story, and stories are for barbecues. A doctor wants symptoms. It was nothing.

"No," says Dennis. "Nothing."

[CASE: Minnesota 2024, the visits] This is not a joke. In the ten days before she was admitted, the woman from Minnesota saw health-care providers about shoulder pain and weakness. Her medical record has no note that she mentioned the bat. Her family told the hospital once she was admitted. A doctor can't read your arm. If Dennis doesn't say the word bat, she'll hear flu. [SCENE: a doctor's desk; a speech bubble above Dennis that stays empty]

So he goes home with some advice about rest. And a few days later, the virus reaches his brain.

Now the problem is a wall. The brain sits behind a barrier that keeps most things out, including the antibodies and immune cells that would hunt the virus down anywhere else. In animal experiments, researchers describe the same picture: the barrier stays shut, the immune response stays outside, and the infection is lethal. Even the body's own antibodies usually don't appear until the second week of illness. By then, there's no time. Rabies doesn't beat the immune system. It goes where the immune system isn't allowed. [DATA: a brain outline, a thick wall around it, small antibody shapes stopped at the edge]

That's why treatment after symptoms isn't the same as treatment before them. After the first signs, the treatment that works so well early on is unsuccessful. [PLATES: the first colour plate drops off Dennis]

Then comes the scene that gave the disease its oldest name. A week later, Dennis is at dinner with his family. He picks up a glass of water, lifts it, and his throat locks. It isn't fear. It's a reflex. The muscles that pull air in contract on their own, and he can't swallow. His family sees a man gripping a glass, eyes wide, shaking. He sees it too. And his mind, at this stage, can still be perfectly clear. He is thirsty, and he is afraid of the one thing that would help. [SCENE: a dinner table; Dennis mostly grey, a trembling glass, the colour layers badly out of register] [PLATES: the second colour plate drops]

The same thing can happen when a draught of air touches his skin. Or a door slams. A breeze becomes a threat. Doctors call it aerophobia. His wife turns off the ceiling fan, then the tap, then the radio. A whole house rearranges itself around one man's throat.

On the right, the same dinner. Dennis finishes his glass of water and asks for another. [SCENE: right-hand Dennis at the same table, in register, a full glass, a smile]

In about eight in ten cases, rabies takes this furious form, with agitation, hallucinations, and fear. In about two in ten, it takes a quieter paralytic form, where muscles weaken slowly, starting at the wound.

Saliva builds up. In hospital, the body's automatic controls start to fail, one by one. Fever. Soaking sweats. Blood pressure that won't hold. In one set of cases that doctors wrote up, the kidneys failed too. [SCENE: a hospital room; a drip stand; the colour layers sliding]

Then delirium. Convulsions. Coma. Without intensive care, it usually comes within seven to ten days. [PLATES: only the navy line plate remains]

There is one exception, and it's worth telling honestly. That fifteen-year-old in Wisconsin was put into a drug-induced coma for seven days, and she recovered. She was the first person known to recover from rabies without a vaccine before or after she got sick. She isn't a method. She's an exception. CDC calls survival after symptoms incredibly rare, and says there is no proven treatment. [SCENE: a hospital bed, a girl's hand, the colour layers returning very slowly] [STILLNESS]

So belief three ends the way the others did. Not with a doctor who couldn't help. With a doctor who could have helped six weeks earlier, if someone had said the word bat.

[WRY]
None of this is new. Every century has had its Dennis. Only ours has Gary.

Democritus described the disease about two thousand four hundred years ago, and for most of that time, there was no vaccine. Then, in July of eighteen eighty-five, a nine-year-old boy named Joseph Meister, bitten fourteen times by a rabid dog, was given Pasteur's experimental vaccine over ten days. He survived. He was, in effect, the first Dennis on the right-hand road, and at nine, he was too young to apologise for bothering anyone. [SCENE: a boy in a nightshirt, a row of syringes, a doctor in a frock coat]

The fix has existed for a hundred and forty years. The World Health Organization still gives two figures for how many people die of rabies each year: about forty-four thousand deaths a year in its latest fact sheet, and fifty-nine thousand in its campaign to end the disease, which works out at one person every nine minutes. Up to ninety-nine percent of those cases come from dogs. About four in ten of the people who die are under fifteen. [DATA: a dot appearing every nine minutes]

The United States shows what the right-hand road looks like at scale. Before nineteen sixty, several hundred people died of rabies every year. Now it's fewer than ten. Every year, about one point four million Americans get checked after a possible exposure, and about a hundred thousand are treated. That means for every person who dies, at least ten thousand are treated, and live. Call them the right-hand Dennises: a hundred thousand a year, thanking nurses and apologising to needles. [DATA: 1.4 million checked, 100,000 treated, under 10 died]

And look at who the few are. Of the three Americans who died in the autumn of twenty twenty-one, none got treatment. Two hadn't realised they were at risk. One was told the bat had tested positive, and still refused the shots, out of a long-standing fear of vaccines. [STILLNESS]

[BUILDING]
So what should Dennis have noticed on day three? Not a symptom. An event.

A bite. A scratch. A lick on broken skin from a wild or unknown animal. Or waking up in a room with a bat. [SCENE: right-hand Dennis, sleeves rolled, at a basin]

Wash the wound with soap and water straight away. The World Health Organization says for at least fifteen minutes. Then see a doctor the same day, and tell them what happened. Say the word. Bat. Dog. Raccoon. The doctor decides what treatment you need. That part isn't for you to guess.

And don't wait for symptoms to make up your mind. CDC says the vaccine can work any time before symptoms begin. Once they begin, it no longer helps. [DATA: a timeline bar; green until "symptoms", then grey]
