## Topic and Day 1 symptom

Rabies after an animal bite or scratch. Day 1 "symptom" is not a symptom at all: a small bite, scratch or lick from a wild or unknown animal, often a bat. The wound heals and nothing hurts. Present-day opening (owner structure override): first vague symptoms weeks later, explained away as flu or stress.

## How the system works (plain words, one analogy)

Your nerves are cables that carry signals from the skin to the brain. The rabies virus doesn't travel in the blood the way a cold does. It enters at the wound and climbs the nerves toward the brain.
Analogy to test: the nerve as a cable, the virus as a slow climber on it. The treatment is a ground crew that gets to the cable before the climber reaches the top.
Source: CDC Yellow Book (virus travels from its point of entry within peripheral nerves to the central nervous system). The speed and "silent for weeks" framing must stay at the level the sources support.

## What goes wrong (mechanism)

1. Virus in saliva enters through a bite, a scratch, or saliva on broken skin or mucous membranes.
2. It travels along peripheral nerves to the central nervous system. No symptoms during this phase.
3. Once in the brain it replicates quickly and causes brain dysfunction.
4. Early symptoms are flu-like, with a tingling, prickling or itching at the bite site. Then anxiety, confusion, agitation, hallucinations. Furious form: hyperactivity, fear of water (hydrophobia), fear of air movement (aerophobia). Paralytic form (about 20% of human cases per WHO): gradual muscle paralysis from the wound site.
5. Coma and death, usually within 7–10 days of the first symptom without intensive care.

## Myths (3 for the narrator's quiz: belief, truth, source)

| # | Belief | Truth | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | "I'd know if a bat bit me." | Bat teeth are tiny; wounds may not be visible or felt. In 12 of 21 US bat-associated cases (1980–1999), there was contact but no detectable bite. | CDC Yellow Book; CDC MMWR (to confirm) |
| 2 | "A rabid animal foams at the mouth and attacks." | Foaming and extreme aggression are frequently not seen, in animals or in humans. You can't tell by looking; only lab testing can. | CDC (animals and rabies) |
| 3 | "I'll wait and see if I get symptoms." | Once symptoms start, vaccine no longer helps and the disease is nearly always fatal. Treatment works before symptoms. | CDC, WHO |

Backup myths: "only dogs spread it" (in the US bats lead; globally dogs cause up to 99% of cases, per WHO), "the shots are many painful needles in the stomach" (modern PEP is wound care, immune globulin, and a short series of arm vaccinations; check wording before using).

## Stage timeline (illustrative; note the real range between patients)

The script says once, plainly, that this timeline is illustrative.
- **Day 0:** the bite. Often unnoticed or dismissed.
- **Weeks to months (WHO: typically 2–3 months, range one week to one year):** no symptoms. The virus is on its way.
- **Prodrome (a few days):** fever, headache, weakness, tingling or itching at the old wound.
- **Neurological phase:** anxiety, confusion, agitation, hallucinations; hydrophobia and aerophobia in furious rabies.
- **Coma and death:** usually within 7–10 days of the first symptom if no intensive care.

Note: WHO says the incubation is "typically 2–3 months but may vary from one week to one year"; CDC says "weeks to months". Use the WHO range and attribute it.

## The Day 3 signs

Not a sign but an event. **Any bite, scratch or lick on broken skin from a wild or unknown animal, or waking to find a bat in the room:** wash the wound with soap and water and see a clinician the same day for post-exposure treatment. Do not wait for symptoms, because that is when it is too late.
The action is to go to a clinician. No self-treatment advice beyond washing the wound, which both WHO and CDC state. Check with the clinical-safety rule: the ending points toward a clinician.

## Numbers for the reality check (each with source)

- Nearly 100% fatal once symptoms appear (WHO: "virtually 100%"; CDC: "nearly always fatal").
- PEP "nearly 100% effective if you get it after exposure" (CDC).
- 44,203 deaths a year worldwide (WHO, 2021 Global Health Estimates).
- Up to 99% of human cases are from dog bites; about 40% of victims are children under 15 (WHO). Child figure to be handled with care: it is a statistic, not a story.
- Fewer than 10 deaths a year in the US (CDC), with around 100,000 Americans getting treatment after a potential exposure each year.
- Survival after symptoms is "incredibly rare" (CDC Yellow Book). One 2004 case is the best-known. The count of documented survivors is disputed and the treatment is not established, so the script does not quote a number.

## What we will NOT claim

- No "cure", no "Milwaukee protocol works". The CDC says there is no evidence-based treatment, and survival is incredibly rare.
- No survivor count. Sources disagree.
- No home remedies, no "I'll be fine if I wash it". Washing helps but is not a substitute for seeing a clinician.
- No "the shots are always needed" for every animal contact: PEP depends on the animal and the situation, which is a clinician's call. The script says "see a clinician", not "get the shots".
- No gore, no graphic frothing or restraint imagery. Hydrophobia is described, not dramatised.
- No claim about the exact speed the virus travels. Sources in hand do not give it.
- No overstating bats: the CDC says at least 7 of 10 US rabies deaths are bat-related, which is a US statistic.
- No children-in-peril story. Dennis is an adult.
