Dennis Fine is forty-three, and he is standing in his kitchen, rubbing his left forearm. It tingles. Not much. Like a foot waking up, except it's an arm, and it isn't waking up. He searches his phone for "tingling arm fever." The first result says it's usually nothing. He stops reading there. That is the entire research project. [SCENE: phone screen, first result highlighted, thumb already moving away]

Then he texts Gary from accounts, whose cousin once had the same thing. Gary says it went away on its own. Dennis finds this deeply comforting. He does not ask what the cousin's thing was.

So Dennis decides to wait it out. It feels like the sensible choice. But the decision that actually mattered is already six weeks behind him, and he doesn't know it yet. [STILLNESS]

[SCENE: hard cut. Full colour. A wooden cabin at dawn. Caption: "Six weeks earlier."]

Same man. Same mug. A rented fishing cabin, five in the morning, and something is flapping around the ceiling. Dennis wakes up, sees a small dark shape, and does what any brave man would do. He hides under the blanket, then throws a towel at it. [SCENE: bat circling a cabin ceiling; Dennis half under a blanket; towel mid-air]

The bat finds the open window and leaves. Dennis checks himself. No blood. No bite marks. Nothing hurts. He tells it as a funny story at breakfast, and everyone laughs. [BUBBLE: "It's probably nothing."]

[BUILDING]
Let's slow this down, because this is the moment the whole story turns on.

What Dennis has just done is wake up in a room with a bat. And in a minute, you'll see why that matters, even with no mark on him. First, three quick questions. No prizes. Dennis would get all three wrong. [SCENE: clean white explainer background; three question tags appear one at a time]

Question one. If a bat bit you, would you know?

Most people say yes. A bite hurts, a bite bleeds, you'd see it. But bats have tiny teeth, and the wounds they leave might not be easy to see at all. Of the twenty-one bat-linked rabies cases in the United States between 1980 and 1999, twelve involved people who had touched or been near a bat but had no bite anyone could find. [DATA: 21 dots, 12 highlighted]

Question two. Which animal gives people rabies?

Everyone says dogs. Across the world, they're right. Dogs are behind up to ninety-nine percent of human cases. But in the United States, it's a different story. At least seven out of ten people who die of rabies there caught it from a bat. [DATA: world map for dogs, US inset for bats]

Question three. If you were infected, how long before you'd feel something?

Hold on to this one. It's the whole video. The answer is typically two to three months. But it can be as short as one week, or as long as a year. [SCENE: a calendar page slowly turning, no marks on it]

So here is the uncomfortable part. For weeks, sometimes months, nothing happens at all.

[BUILDING]
Now the ninety-second version of what rabies actually is.

Think of your nerves as cables. They run from your skin all the way to your brain, carrying signals up and down. That's their only job. [SCENE: white background, a single cable running from a hand to a brain, soft grey]

Rabies is a virus that hides in saliva. If that saliva reaches a bite, a scratch, or a damaged bit of skin, the virus gets in. And then it doesn't spread through your blood like a cold. It climbs. From the wound, along the nerve, toward the brain. [DATA: the cable highlights in clinical red, a slow dot climbing] 

The whole way up, you feel nothing. No fever, no ache. Your body doesn't raise an alarm, because from the outside, nothing is happening. [STILLNESS]

Then the virus reaches the brain, and it multiplies fast. That is when symptoms begin, and that is when it is too late.

Here's the part that matters most. Treatment after a possible exposure works by stopping the virus from reaching the central nervous system. It has to get to the cable before the climber does. Once the climber is at the top, there's nothing left to stop. [SCENE: a second figure on the cable, intercepting the dot near the bottom]

So back to the cabin. Dennis is at the window with a towel in his hand. And this is where the story splits.

[SCENE: split screen; left side labelled "Ignoring-Dennis", right side "Goes-on-Day-3 Dennis"; both identical for one beat]

On the left, Dennis finishes his fishing weekend. He drives home. He puts the bat story into his rotation of funny stories. By the following week, he has forgotten about it entirely.

On the right, a different Dennis. Same cabin, same towel. But that evening, a small voice says, wait, did that bat touch me? So on the third day, he phones a clinic. And he apologises. He says sorry for bothering them. He says it's probably nothing. [SCENE: right-side Dennis on a phone, hand over his eyes] [BUBBLE: "I'm so sorry to bother you."]

So he gets treated. The treatment has three parts. The wound care, if there's anything to clean. A shot of antibodies that gives his body a head start. And a series of four vaccine doses over two weeks. His arm is sore. He's annoyed about the afternoon off work. That's all. [SCENE: clinic, sore arm, a bandage, Dennis checking his watch]

A quick note first. This timeline is illustrative. Real patients vary widely, from one week to a year. Dennis's version sits in the middle.

For the first few weeks, nothing happens. Nothing at all. His colours are still on. He grills at the weekend. He gets a promotion. This is the cruel part of the disease. [BUILDING]
A few days later, the tingling is no longer the main problem.

The early flu-like phase gives way to something harder. Anxiety that has no reason. Confusion. Agitation. Hallucinations. In one form, there can also be a fear of water, and a fear of moving air. This is the virus in the brain. [DATA: "Week 7" tag; grade falls toward grey on the left]

About one in five human cases takes a different, quieter route, with muscles slowly weakening, starting at the wound. Either way, the road ends in the same place. [DATA: "paralytic form: 20%"]

And it ends quickly. Without intensive care, death usually follows within seven to ten days of that first symptom. [SCENE: left-side Dennis's room, fully grey; a window, a chair, no one in frame] [STILLNESS]

We are not going to linger there. There's nothing in that room that teaches you anything.

[WRY]
So what does it feel like? In the beginning, almost nothing. A tingle. A tired afternoon. A headache that has an explanation. If you're the person it's happening to, you don't feel like someone in danger. You feel like someone who needs an early night.

And that's the trap. The disease doesn't announce itself. It hides in the shape of a normal week.

[BUILDING]
Now, the reality check. Each of these numbers is on the screen once, and then we move on. [DATA: "Reality Check" tag]

Once symptoms appear, rabies is virtually always fatal. Survival after that point is incredibly rare, and there is no proven treatment. [DATA: big "Almost always fatal"]

Around the world, rabies kills about forty-four thousand people a year. [DATA: 44,000]

In the United States, fewer than ten people die of it each year. In the same country, about a hundred thousand Americans get treated after a possible exposure, every single year. [DATA: "< 10 deaths" next to "~100,000 treated"]

And treatment after an exposure is nearly a hundred percent effective, if it's given before symptoms. [DATA: green tick on the right-hand Dennis]

Look at what those numbers say together. The disease is nearly unbeatable once it shows itself, and nearly harmless if you act before it does. The whole difference is when you go.

[WRY]
So here is what Dennis should have noticed on day three. And it isn't a symptom. It's an event.

A bite. A scratch. A lick on broken skin from a wild or unknown animal. Or waking up in a room with a bat, even when you can't find a mark. Any of these is a reason to go.

Wash the wound with soap and water straight away. The World Health Organization says for at least fifteen minutes. Then see a doctor the same day, and tell them what happened. The doctor decides what treatment you need. That part is not for you to guess. [SCENE: right-side Dennis, sleeves rolled up, washing his forearm, a clock behind him]

And don't wait for a symptom to make up your mind. Remember question three? The vaccine can work any time before symptoms begin. Once they begin, it no longer helps. [DATA: a timeline bar; green until "symptoms", then grey]

This video is educational. It is not medical advice, and it cannot tell you what to do about a particular animal or a particular bite. A doctor can.
