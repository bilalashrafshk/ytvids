## Spine

Dennis believes three things after the bat leaves. Each one is wrong, and each is wrong in a way that is worse than the one before:

1. **"I'd know if it bit me."** (No: the bite can be invisible. Real people did not recognise their exposure.)
2. **"If it were serious, I'd feel something soon."** (No: the quiet stretch is the dangerous stretch; the virus waits near the bite, then rides the nerve's own delivery system to the brain.)
3. **"And if it gets bad, doctors can fix it."** (No: once symptoms start, the treatment that works before them no longer works. The immune system arrives at a sealed building.)

The video follows the Ignoring road through those three beliefs, cutting to the Day-3 Dennis at each one. A real case that echoes each belief lands in each act: people who did exactly what Dennis did.

## Mechanism (plain words, our own images)

1. **Entry.** Saliva reaches a bite, scratch, or broken skin or mucous membrane (WHO, CDC).
2. **The wait at the door.** The virus is at or near the bite for most of the incubation period (Jackson 2011). Infection of muscle fibres at the site is thought to be a critical step toward the nerves (cited in CDC/review sources).
3. **The ride.** It enters nerve endings and travels by retrograde fast axonal transport, the nerve's own delivery system, using the nerve cell's machinery (Gluska 2014; Jackson 2011). In cultured neurons the virus has been timed at about 0.93 micrometres per second (lab finding; tier C if used).
4. **Not through the blood.** "There is still no credible evidence that hematogenous spread of rabies virus to the central nervous system plays a significant role" (Jackson 2011).
5. **The wall.** In the brain the barrier stays intact, circulating antibodies cannot get in, and treatment given after signs appear is unsuccessful (Hooper 2009). Antibodies are generally not formed before the second week of illness (Wertheim 2009).
6. **Symptoms.** Tingling, itching or pain at the old bite and flu-like illness first (CDC). Then anxiety, confusion, agitation, hallucinations (CDC). Furious (encephalitic) form in about 80% of cases, paralytic about 20% (Jackson 2011; WHO says about 20% paralytic). About 50–80% of patients develop hydrophobia: attempts to drink cause reflex contraction of the inspiratory muscles and an inability to swallow; air flow (aerophobia) and noise can do the same (Jackson 2011; Wertheim 2009). In the two Vietnamese patients, consciousness was intact on arrival (Glasgow Coma Score 15), with sweating, fever to 39 °C, low blood pressure and kidney failure (Wertheim 2009). Excess saliva appears in CDC's 2021 Texas report.
7. **The end.** Without intensive care, death usually follows within 7 to 10 days of the first symptom (Yellow Book). The three 2021 US patients died about 2 to 3 weeks after symptom onset (CDC MMWR).

## The ancient and the modern (history, short, each sourced)

- **Ancient:** Democritus (460–370 BCE) described it; Hippocrates is believed to refer to people who "drink very little, are disturbed and frightened, tremble at the least noise" (CDC *Etymologia: Rabies*). The word comes from Latin *rabere*, to rage.
- **1885:** Joseph Meister, nine years old, bitten 14 times by a rabid dog, treated with Pasteur's vaccine over ten days, and survived (Institut Pasteur).
- **Before 1960:** several hundred US deaths a year. Now fewer than ten (CDC).

## Real cases

- **Minnesota, 2024.** A woman found a bat in her sink in July and killed it with a hammer. Weeks later, shoulder pain and weakness. She never mentioned the bat during multiple visits in her first nine days of illness. The family disclosed it after she was hospitalised. She died 12 days after admission. The bat was thrown away untested.
- **California, 2024.** A woman handling an apparently dead bat with bare hands felt movement and a possible bite. About a month later, tingling and muscle spasms in the arm. She did not consult a provider. She died 4 days after admission.
- **United States, 2021.** Three deaths. Two did not recognise their risk; one refused treatment after a positive bat test because of fears about the vaccine. All had contact with a bat 3 to 7 weeks before symptoms; all died 2 to 3 weeks after symptoms began.
- **Wisconsin, 2004.** A 15-year-old bitten on the finger by a bat at a church; the 5 mm wound was cleaned with hydrogen peroxide; no treatment sought. About a month later, tingling and numbness in the hand. Kept in a drug-induced coma for 7 days, she became the first person known to recover without receiving a vaccine before or after illness. CDC still calls survival "incredibly rare", with no evidence-based treatment.
- Held back by choice (not by rule): the 7-year-old's 2021 Texas case. We use the adult cases; the child is a counted statistic only.

## Numbers for the reality check

- WHO gives two figures: about 44,000 deaths a year (fact sheet, 2021 estimate) and 59,000, one person every nine minutes (United Against Rabies campaign page). The script states both. WHO's goal: zero human dog-mediated rabies deaths by 2030.
- Up to 99% of human cases come from dogs; about 40% of victims are under 15 (WHO).
- US: fewer than 10 deaths a year; 1.4 million people get care for a possible exposure and 100,000 get treatment each year; before 1960, several hundred deaths a year (CDC).
- Among 42 US-acquired cases 2000–2024, 35 (83%) were bat contact (CDC MMWR 2026).
- PEP is nearly 100% effective if received before symptoms (CDC); no failures documented in the US since current biologics were licensed (ACIP 2008).

## The Day-3 sign

An event, not a symptom: a bite, scratch, or lick on broken skin from a wild or unknown animal, or waking up with a bat in the room. Wash the wound with soap and water (WHO: at least 15 minutes) and see a doctor the same day, **and tell them about the animal**. CDC: PEP is recommended regardless of how long ago the exposure was, as long as it is before symptoms.

## What we will not claim

- A survivor total, a "cure", or that the Milwaukee protocol works.
- A speed for how fast the virus moves through a living person (only the cultured-neuron lab figure, labelled).
- Treatment advice beyond washing the wound and seeing a doctor. The doctor decides the treatment.
- That the virus "plans" to make hosts bite. Behavioural changes that favour transmission are described by researchers; the script says "the effect is that", not "the virus wants".
- Any named patient. Real cases are anonymised.
