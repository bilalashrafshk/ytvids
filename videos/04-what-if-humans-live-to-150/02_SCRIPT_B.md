# Draft B — "You" Through the Changed World (A5) — Swing

- **Channel:** FinanceCraft · Track 2 (The Hypothetical)
- **Skeleton:** A5 Constrained Hypothetical, used as a loose guide — "you" is the lens, the world is the subject
- **Premise as locked:** same as Draft A. You are born into the world where everyone lives to 150.
- **The meter:** $40,000 a year to be alive, today's dollars, no growth. 150 × 40,000 = $6,000,000. Ledger checkpoints below all follow from that rate.
- **Guard against the old failure:** each time block visits a different part of the world (family, work, homes, money, power, death) — the meter is the clock, not the topic.

---

[WRY]
You were born into a world where everyone lives to one hundred and fifty. It isn't a longer old age. It's a longer everything. Here are the rules. You don't get old for about a century. Nobody retires early. And nobody above you is leaving. There's one catch. It's a meter. Being alive costs about forty thousand dollars a year. For one hundred and fifty years. That's six million dollars. Run out early, and you live the rest on nothing. [DATA: the meter — $40,000 a year × 150 = $6,000,000]

[WATERMARK: HYPOTHETICAL SCENARIO]

[BUILDING]
Let's start the clock. [REMOTION: ARCHETYPE_WHIP_ZOOM_MONTAGE — birthday cakes whipping past, candle counts climbing 1 → 150]

You're six. It's your birthday. There are forty people at your party. Thirty of them are your family. Your parents. Your grandparents. Their parents. And their parents too. [SCENE: six-year-old's party, the room full of ancestors]

The oldest one is a hundred and twenty-six. He's on the bouncy castle.

You look around for other kids. There are two. You're rare. In your world, there are half as many children as there used to be. There has to be. Otherwise the planet runs out of room. [DATA: children — 1 in 4 people today, 1 in 8 here]

Somebody takes a family photo. You're in the front row. It's the only row with space in it.

[BUILDING]
You're twenty. You get your first job. You open your first payslip. Near the bottom, there's a new line.

Years to retirement. Ninety-four. [SCENE: payslip line "Years to retirement: 94"]

You laugh. It isn't a joke.

Here's why. Your grandparents' world let you stop at sixty-five. In your world, that would leave you eighty-five years with no pay. Nobody can save for that. So the pension age moved. It's one hundred and twenty now. [DATA: retire at 65 → 85 unpaid years; at 120 → 30]

The meter doesn't care. Your parents paid for your first twenty years. You still owe the rest.

The meter: five million, two hundred thousand dollars still to pay. [DATA: ledger — age 20: $5,200,000]

[WATERMARK: HYPOTHETICAL SCENARIO]

[WRY]
You're thirty. You want a home.

You walk down Marrow Street. Forty houses. Each front door is painted with the year it was bought. None of them is less than ninety years old. Nobody's selling. Nobody's leaving. [MAP: Marrow Street, every door year 90+ years old]

Back then, about one home in forty-five came up for sale each year because someone died. Now it's one in a hundred and fifteen. And there are twice as many people wanting one. [DATA: homes freed each year — 1 in 45 vs 1 in 115]

The bank offers you a deal. A hundred-year mortgage. It sounds cheaper. It isn't. Your monthly payment drops by less than three hundred dollars. But you'll pay three and a half times as much interest. [DATA: 30-year vs 100-year — $1,799 vs $1,504 a month; interest ~$350K vs ~$1.2M]

You sign anyway. Where else would you live?

Every month, a letter arrives. Payment one of one thousand, two hundred.

[TENSE]
You're forty-five. Money's tight. So you do what everyone does.

You sell your inheritance. Not the house. The right to it. Your mother is seventy-five, fit as a fiddle, and she'll live another seventy-five years. So you'd get her house at one hundred and twenty. A bank gives you cash for it now. [SCENE: signing over the future house]

Now the bank owns your mother's house. It just has to wait. Every year, on her birthday, it sends her a card. It asks how she's feeling.

She's feeling great. That's the problem.

[WATERMARK: HYPOTHETICAL SCENARIO]

[STILLNESS]
You're seventy. In the old world, this is the year you'd die.

Your body feels about forty-five.

The meter: three million, two hundred thousand dollars still to pay. [DATA: ledger — age 70: $3,200,000]

Stop and look at that. You've lived a whole old-world life. And you still owe more than half.

[BUILDING]
So you go back to school. Everyone does. Whatever you learned at twenty stopped being useful around the time you turned sixty.

You sit in a lecture hall. The kid next to you is eighteen. Same course. Same exam. You have fifty years of work left. She has a hundred. Your CV is eleven pages long. Hers is a single line. [COMPOSITE: the 18-year-old classmate]

[TENSE]
You're ninety. You want the top job.

So does the man on the bench outside the corner office. He's been waiting forty years. He wears the same suit. He asks the same question, once a decade.

"Is he in?" [COMPOSITE: the man outside the corner office]

He's in. He's been in since before you were born.

Back when people left at eighty, a big company's top job came free every seven years or so. Now, a boss can stay for forty. Take the five hundred biggest jobs in the country. About seventy used to open up a year. Now it's twelve. [DATA: top jobs opening each year — 71 vs 12]

The meter: two million, four hundred thousand dollars still to pay. [DATA: ledger — age 90: $2,400,000]

So you do what your whole generation does. You quit. You start something new. It's the only place with no queue. And every election, you vote for the same thing. Term limits. On everything. [SCENE: ballot, "TERM LIMITS" ticked]

[WATERMARK: HYPOTHETICAL SCENARIO]

[STILLNESS]
You're one hundred and thirteen. Something changes.

Your knees hand in their notice. You get tired on stairs. You're finally getting old. Not for fifteen years, like your ancestors. For thirty-seven. [DATA: old age — ~15 years then, ~37 now]

The thing most likely to kill you still isn't age. It's a car. A crash at forty used to cost forty years. Now it costs a hundred and ten. You drive very, very carefully.

[BUILDING]
You're one hundred and twenty. Two things happen in the same month.

You retire.

And your mother dies. At one hundred and fifty, peacefully, in the garden.

The house was never yours. You sold it seventy-five years ago. The bank sends a final card. It doesn't ask how anyone is feeling.

The meter: one million, two hundred thousand dollars still to pay. [DATA: ledger — age 120: $1,200,000]

That has to be in the bank, today. Thirty years of life, with no more paydays. If you saved about twelve thousand a year for a hundred years, you're fine. If you didn't, there's no one left to inherit from. [DATA: $12,000 a year × 100 working years = $1,200,000]

[WATERMARK: HYPOTHETICAL SCENARIO]

[STILLNESS]
You're one hundred and fifty.

The family photo is five rows deep now. You're in the back row. There's a new baby on the floor at the front. She's the only one down there. [SCENE: five-row family photo, one baby in the front row]

At St Aldric's, half the building is a crematorium. The other half is a nursery. One chimney. One playground. One car park. [SCENE: St Aldric's, half crematorium, half nursery]

The undertaker's apprentice has been training for sixty years. You're about to be his first.

The meter: zero. [DATA: ledger — age 150: $0]

[STILLNESS]
You always thought a life was a ladder. You'd climb it, you'd reach the top, and then it'd be your turn.

It was always a queue. And the only thing that ever moved it was death.
