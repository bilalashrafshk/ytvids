- **Skeleton:** A12 World Cascade, used as a loose guide
- **Premise as locked:** ageing slows for everyone, starting from the body they already have. People alive today gain some years (a 40-year-old reaches ~129, a 70-year-old ~92); babies born from day one are the first to reach 150. People grow up in 20 years as now, get old from around 113, and generations stay about 30 years apart.

[WRY]
Imagine a birthday party. The girl is six. There are forty people in the room, and thirty of them are her parents, her grandparents, and the grandparents of her grandparents. The oldest guest is a hundred and twenty-six. He is not the one in the wheelchair. He's the one on the bouncy castle. [COMPOSITE: great-great-grandfather]

[BUILDING]
This is what the world looks like when humans live to one hundred and fifty. And no, it isn't a world of very old people. It's a world where nobody leaves. By the end, you'll see why, in this world, the healthiest mother on the street is her children's biggest problem.

[WATERMARK: HYPOTHETICAL SCENARIO]

It starts quietly. One morning, ageing slows down, for everyone, from wherever their body already is. A man blows out forty candles, same as last year. He'll now make it to about a hundred and thirty. His father, who's seventy, gets twenty-odd bonus years. And the baby born in the hospital across town that morning? She's the first human who will ever see one hundred and fifty. [SCENE: newborn in a hospital cot, name card on the crib]

Across town, at a pension company, someone changes one number on a spreadsheet. Eighty becomes one hundred and fifty. Every cell below it turns red. [DATA: one cell changes from 80 to 150, the column below turns red]

[STILLNESS]
Because here's the thing we never notice. The whole world is built around people leaving.

You work for about forty years, then you stop. Your parents pass away, and in your fifties, you get the house. The boss retires, and somebody gets promoted. Every few decades, the jobs, the homes, the money and the power all come free again. Death is the thing that moves the queue.

Take that away, and the queue stops moving. And the first place you notice is the family photo.

[WRY]
Today, a big family photo has three rows. Grandparents at the back, parents in the middle, kids on the floor. In this world, you retake it every twenty years, and every time, the frame gets wider. By the time that baby from the hospital is a great-great-grandmother, it's five rows deep. Five generations, all alive, all healthy, all arguing about where to sit. [SCENE: family photo reshot, the frame widening each time]

And look at the front row. It's almost empty.

If everyone lives twice as long and keeps having kids at today's pace, the planet runs out of room. So families shrink. The world grows to about nineteen billion people, roughly double today, and then it stops. Children go from one in every four people to one in eight. [DATA: long run — ~19.5 billion people, then flat; under-20s 1 in 4 → 1 in 8]

A kid in this world is rare. She is outnumbered by her own ancestors at her own birthday party.

[BUILDING]
And if the family photo gets longer, so does the marriage in the middle of it.

Today, "till death do us part" means about fifty years. Here, marry at thirty, and death is a hundred and twenty years away. That's a very long time to agree on a thermostat. [SCENE: an old couple, both young-looking, arguing at the thermostat]

So the vows change. Couples start writing an end date into them. At the jeweller's, wedding rings come engraved with a year, the way milk comes with a date. And Grandma's third wedding is a lovely day. Her first husband gives a speech. [SCENE: wedding ring engraved with an end date] [COMPOSITE: Grandma's first husband, giving a toast]

[BUILDING]
Longer marriages are a choice. Longer careers aren't.

Today, you start work at twenty, stop at sixty-five, and have about fifteen years left. Here, stopping at sixty-five would leave you eighty-five years with no pay. Nobody can save for that. So the retirement age has to move, and once you do the sums, it lands at about one hundred and twenty. [DATA: retire at 65 → 85 unpaid years; retire at 120 → 30]

Picture your first payslip at twenty. Near the bottom, a new line: years to retirement, ninety-four. [SCENE: payslip line "Years to retirement: 94"]

But a hundred-year career has a problem. Whatever you learned at twenty is useless by sixty. So people go back to school, again and again. In a lecture hall, a seventy-year-old sits next to an eighteen-year-old. Same course. Same exam. The seventy-year-old has fifty more years of work ahead. The eighteen-year-old has a hundred. The seventy-year-old's CV is eleven pages long. [COMPOSITE: 70-year-old student]

[WATERMARK: HYPOTHETICAL SCENARIO]

[WRY]
And a hundred years of work means a hundred years of paying for a home.

You'd think time would make everyone rich. Give your savings a century and a half to grow, and a small pot becomes a fortune. But everyone has the same idea, all at once. Open a savings book in this world and watch the rate on each new page. It keeps getting smaller. [SCENE: savings passbook, 40 pages, the interest rate shrinking page by page]

So people borrow instead, and the banks invent the hundred-year mortgage. It sounds cheap. It isn't. On a three hundred thousand dollar house, the monthly payment drops by less than three hundred dollars. But you pay three and a half times as much interest. You're not really paying off the house. You're renting it from the bank, for the rest of your very long life. [DATA: 30-year vs 100-year mortgage — $1,799 vs $1,504 a month; total interest ~$350K vs ~$1.2M]

A letter arrives. Payment eight hundred and forty-seven of one thousand, two hundred. [SCENE: mortgage letter "Payment 847 of 1,200"]

[BUILDING]
Which is why everyone is waiting for the other way to get a house. The one you inherit.

Today, you get your parents' home in your fifties. Here, your parents live to one hundred and fifty. They're thirty years older than you. So you get the house at one hundred and twenty. [DATA: inherit at ~50 today → ~120]

Picture the queue for one house: every heir waiting for someone to die, stretching down the street and round the block. [REMOTION: ARCHETYPE_INFINITE_PORTAL_TUNNEL — endless queue of heirs outside one front door]

Walk down Marrow Street. Forty houses. Each front door is painted with the year it was bought. Not one of them has changed hands in ninety years. Today, about one home in forty-five comes free each year when somebody dies. Here, it's one in a hundred and fifteen. And there are twice as many people who need one. [MAP: Marrow Street, every door year 90+ years old] [DATA: homes freed each year — 1 in 45 vs 1 in 115]

A brass key hangs inside one of those doors. It has a paper tag, for writing each owner's name. It's had one name on it for a very long time. [SCENE: brass key, paper tag with one name]

So the young do something clever, and a little bit dark. At twenty-five, they sell their future inheritance to a bank, for cash today. The bank now owns the right to Mum's house. And every year, on Mum's birthday, the bank sends her a card. It asks how she's feeling. [SCENE: bank birthday card — "How are you feeling this year?"]

[TENSE]
The same queue that blocks the front door blocks the top floor.

On the fortieth floor of the Greyfield Exchange, the desk nameplates are engraved in brass. Nobody prints them anymore. Nobody has moved desks in eighty years. [SCENE: brass nameplates, same names for 80 years]

Today, a top boss keeps the job for about seven years. Here, the same boss can stay for forty. Take five hundred of the biggest jobs in a country. Today, about seventy of them open up every year. Here, it's about twelve. [DATA: top jobs opening each year — 71 vs 12]

Outside one corner office, a man in a sharp suit sits on a bench. He's ninety-one. He's been waiting for this job since he was fifty. Every ten years, he asks the same thing.

"Is he in?" [COMPOSITE: the man outside the corner office]

He's in.

The same goes for judges, for professors, for anyone who holds a seat until they choose to leave. In a courthouse hallway, six portraits hang side by side. It's the same judge. Each one is a little greyer. [SCENE: six portraits of one judge]

So ambition leaves. If you can't climb the old ladder, you build a new one. Every founder is under forty, because only a brand new company has no queue. And the one thing young people vote for, over and over, is term limits. On everything. [SCENE: protest sign "TERM LIMITS FOR EVERYTHING"]

[STILLNESS]
Now, to be fair, some of this is wonderful. A surgeon with ninety years of practice. A teacher who taught your grandmother, and now teaches you, and still remembers what your grandmother was like at your age. Longer lives have always made the world richer. That part is true. [SCENE: an old-young teacher at a chalkboard, class photo of three generations on the wall]

But only if the old let go. And that's where it turns upside down.

Remember the bank's birthday card? Every year it asks Mum how she's feeling. And every year, she writes back: never better. [SCENE: stack of birthday cards, each answered "Never better!"]

Every one of those cards is another year her children rent. In this world, your parents' long, happy, healthy life is your single biggest bill. It's the one thing nobody says out loud at the birthday party.

[BUILDING]
And then there's death itself. It doesn't vanish. It changes shape.

You still get old. It just starts around one hundred and thirteen. Your knees hand in their notice. You slow down for your last thirty-odd years, instead of your last fifteen. [DATA: old age — ~15 years today vs ~37 at 150]

But the biggest killer isn't age anymore. It's the road. A car crash at forty used to cost forty years. Now it costs one hundred and ten. People start driving like they have a lot to lose. Because they do.

St Aldric's crematorium only needs half its building now. The other half becomes a nursery. One chimney. One playground. One car park. [SCENE: St Aldric's, half crematorium, half nursery]

And the undertaker's apprentice has been training for sixty years. He's never actually seen a body. [COMPOSITE: undertaker's apprentice]

Remember the girl at the birthday party?

She's one hundred and twenty now. She has a job, a mortgage, and a son who's ninety. This morning, her mother died, peacefully, in the garden, at one hundred and fifty.

She walks up to a door on Marrow Street. The brass key is still hanging inside. Under her mother's name, on a tag that has waited a hundred and fifteen years, she writes a second one. Her own. [SCENE: the key tag, a second name being written]

[STILLNESS]
We like to think a life is a ladder. You start at the bottom, you climb, you reach the top.

It was always a queue. And the only thing that ever moved it was death.
