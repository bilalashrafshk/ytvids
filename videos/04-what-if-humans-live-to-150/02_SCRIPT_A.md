# Draft A — World Cascade (A12, provisional) — Safe

- **Channel:** FinanceCraft · Track 2 (The Hypothetical)
- **Skeleton:** A12 World Cascade, used as a loose guide
- **Premise as locked:** ageing slows for everyone born from day one. People grow up in 20 years as now, stay young for about a century, get old from around 113, and die around 150. Generations stay about 30 years apart.
- **Numbers corrected from the brief:** 100-year mortgage interest ≈ $1.2M total (not $1.5M); max living ancestors at a child's party = 30 (not 62).

---

[WRY]
Imagine a birthday party. The girl is six. There are forty people in the room, and thirty of them are her parents, her grandparents, and the grandparents of her grandparents. The oldest guest is a hundred and twenty-six. He is not the one in the wheelchair. He's the one on the bouncy castle. [COMPOSITE: great-great-grandfather]

[BUILDING]
This is what a world looks like when humans live to one hundred and fifty. And no, it isn't a world of very old people. It's a world where nobody leaves.

[WATERMARK: HYPOTHETICAL SCENARIO]

On day one, nothing happens. That's the strange part. A man blows out forty candles, same as last year. Nobody feels different. But across town, at a pension company, someone changes one number on a spreadsheet. Eighty becomes one hundred and fifty. And every cell below it turns red. [DATA: one cell changes from 80 to 150, the column below turns red]

[STILLNESS]
Because here's the thing we never notice. The whole world is built around people leaving.

You work for about forty years, then you stop. Your parents pass away, and in your fifties, you get the house. The boss retires, and somebody gets promoted. Every few decades, the jobs, the homes, the money and the power all come free again. Death is the thing that moves the queue.

Take that away, and the queue stops moving. Let's walk through what breaks, in the order it breaks.

[WRY]
Start with the family photo.

Today, a big family photo has three rows. Grandparents at the back, parents in the middle, kids on the floor. In this world, you retake the photo every twenty years, and every time, the frame gets wider. By year one hundred, it's five rows deep. Five generations, all alive, all healthy, all arguing about where to sit. [SCENE: family photo reshot, the frame widening each time]

And look at the front row. It's almost empty.

Here's why. If you live twice as long and have the same number of kids, the planet fills up. So people have kids more slowly, or the world runs out of room. In the long run, children go from a quarter of everyone to about one in eight. [DATA: share of people under 20 — 1 in 4 today, 1 in 8 at 150]

A kid in this world is rare. She is outnumbered by her own ancestors at her own birthday party.

[BUILDING]
Now go to work.

Today, a career is about forty years. You start at twenty, you stop at sixty-five, and you have maybe fifteen years left. In this world, stopping at sixty-five isn't a retirement. It's a very long holiday with no money. You'd have eighty-five years left to pay for.

So the pension age moves. It has to. And once you do the sums, the new retirement age lands at about one hundred and twenty. [DATA: retire at 65 → 85 years unpaid; retire at 120 → 30 years]

Picture your first payslip at twenty. Near the bottom, a line reads: years to retirement, ninety-four. [SCENE: payslip line "Years to retirement: 94"]

And a hundred-year career has a problem nobody talks about. Whatever you learned at twenty is useless by sixty. So people go back to school. Again and again. In a lecture hall, a seventy-year-old sits next to an eighteen-year-old. Same course. Same exam. The seventy-year-old has fifty more years of work ahead. The eighteen-year-old has a hundred. The seventy-year-old's CV is eleven pages long. [COMPOSITE: 70-year-old student]

[WATERMARK: HYPOTHETICAL SCENARIO]

[WRY]
Next, money. And at first, it looks like great news.

Put ten thousand dollars away at twenty, let it grow at five percent a year, and by one hundred and fifty, it's worth over five million. Everyone's a millionaire. Right? [DATA: $10,000 growing for 130 years at 5% → about $5.7 million]

Not quite. Because everyone is doing the same thing. Billions of people are saving for a very long life, all chasing the same companies, the same buildings, the same land. When everyone wants to lend and nobody needs to borrow, money earns less. That five percent shrinks. How far, nobody knows. But nobody gets rich just by waiting.

Borrowing changes too. Banks start selling hundred-year mortgages. Sounds cheap. It isn't. On a three hundred thousand dollar house, the monthly payment drops by less than three hundred dollars. But you pay three and a half times as much interest. You're not really paying off the house anymore. You're renting it from the bank, for the rest of your very long life. [DATA: 30-year vs 100-year mortgage — monthly payment $1,799 vs $1,504; total interest ~$350K vs ~$1.2M]

A mortgage statement arrives. Payment eight hundred and forty-seven of one thousand, two hundred. [SCENE: mortgage letter "Payment 847 of 1,200"]

[BUILDING]
Which brings us to the house you were supposed to inherit.

Today, most people get their parents' home in their fifties. Your parents are about thirty years older than you, and they live to about eighty. In this world, they live to one hundred and fifty. So you get the house at one hundred and twenty. [DATA: inherit at ~50 today → ~120 at 150]

Picture the queue for one of these houses: every heir waiting for someone to die, stretching down the street and round the block. [REMOTION: ARCHETYPE_INFINITE_PORTAL_TUNNEL — endless queue of heirs outside one front door]

Walk down Marrow Street. Forty houses. Each front door is painted with the year it was bought. Not one of them has changed hands in ninety years. [MAP: Marrow Street, door years all 90+ years old]

Today, about one home in forty-five comes free every year when somebody dies. In this world, it's one in a hundred and fifteen. Less than half as many. And you need almost twice as many homes, because twice as many people are alive. [DATA: homes freed each year — 1 in 45 today vs 1 in 115]

So the young do something clever, and a little bit dark. At twenty-five, they sell their future inheritance to a bank, for cash today. The bank now owns the right to Mum's house. And every year, on her birthday, the bank sends her a card. It asks how she's feeling. [SCENE: bank birthday card — "How are you feeling this year?"]

[WATERMARK: HYPOTHETICAL SCENARIO]

[TENSE]
Now we get to the part that actually runs the world. Who's in charge.

On the fortieth floor of the Greyfield Exchange, the desk nameplates are engraved in brass. Nobody prints them anymore. Nobody has moved desks in eighty years. [SCENE: brass nameplates, same names for 80 years]

Today, a top boss keeps the job for about seven years. Then someone new gets a turn. In this world, the same boss can stay for forty. Take five hundred of the biggest jobs in a country. Today, about seventy of them open up every year. At one hundred and fifty, it's about twelve. Your chance of getting to the top falls about six times. [DATA: top jobs opening each year — 71 today vs 12]

Outside one corner office, a man in a sharp suit sits on a bench. He's ninety-one. He's been waiting for this job since he was fifty. Every ten years, he asks the same thing.

"Is he in?" [COMPOSITE: the man outside the corner office]

He's in.

The same is true for judges, for professors, for anyone who holds a seat until they choose to leave. In a courthouse hallway, six portraits hang side by side. It's the same judge. Each one is a little greyer. [SCENE: six portraits of one judge]

So ambition leaves. If you can't climb the old ladder, you build a new one. Every founder is under forty, because only a brand new company has no queue. And the one thing young people vote for, over and over, is term limits. On everything. [SCENE: protest sign "TERM LIMITS FOR EVERYTHING"]

[STILLNESS]
Now, you could say this is all fine. Longer lives have always made the world richer. More experience, more time to build things, more grandparents to babysit. And that's true.

But only if the old let go. And the strange thing is, in this world, good health is bad news. Every year your mother stays healthy is another year you rent. Your parents' long, happy life is your worst financial problem. That's the part nobody wants to say out loud at the birthday party.

[BUILDING]
And then there's death itself. It doesn't vanish. It just changes shape.

You still get old. It starts around one hundred and thirteen. Your knees hand in their notice. You slow down for your last thirty-odd years, instead of your last fifteen. [DATA: old age — ~15 years today vs ~37 years at 150]

But the biggest killer isn't age anymore. It's the road. A car crash at forty used to cost forty years. Now it costs one hundred and ten. People start driving like they have a lot to lose. Because they do.

St Aldric's crematorium only needs half its building now. The other half becomes a nursery. One chimney. One playground. One car park. [SCENE: St Aldric's, half crematorium, half nursery]

And the undertaker's apprentice has been training for sixty years. He's never actually seen a body. [COMPOSITE: undertaker's apprentice]

[WATERMARK: HYPOTHETICAL SCENARIO]

[STILLNESS]
Jump ahead a century.

The world settles. About nineteen billion people, and then it stops growing. Births run at half today's rate. Every family photo is five rows deep. Everyone has had three careers. Every important job has a time limit. Houses stay in the same family for centuries, and the oldest people hold most of the money, simply because they had the longest to save it. [DATA: long run — ~19.5 billion people, then flat]

A brass house key hangs by the door of one Marrow Street home. There's a paper tag on it, for writing down each owner's name. After one hundred and twenty years, the tag still has room. It only has one name on it. [SCENE: brass key, paper tag with one name]

[STILLNESS]
We like to think a life is a ladder. You start at the bottom, you climb, you reach the top.

It was always a queue. And the only thing that ever moved it was death.
