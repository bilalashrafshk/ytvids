# What If Humans Lived to 150? Research Pack

## PART 1: Five premises

1. **Constraint twist:** Everyone lives to 150, but the pension law still retires you at 65. That leaves 85 years on a pension built for 15.
2. **Wrong protagonist:** A family of undertakers whose next paying customer isn't due for 70 years.
3. **Inversion:** In year one, an actuary tells her pension fund it is already broke. She is fired. She lives long enough to watch it collapse 60 years later, and she is still unemployed.
4. **Absurd literalisation:** Every heir stands in a real queue outside the house they will inherit. By year 90, the queue for one terraced house wraps round the block. Everyone in it is waiting for someone to die.
5. **Compressed clock:** One 150-year life in one week. Each day is about 21 years. By Tuesday afternoon you would already be dead in the old world. You still have five days left.

**Strongest: 5, the compressed clock.** It makes 150 years something you can grasp, and it can carry both drafts. The other four work better as scenes inside it.

- **1** is one mechanic, the pension. It dies after one act.
- **2** is funny but narrow. It becomes a video about the funeral trade.
- **3** is a person's story. It pulls the tour down to one career.
- **4** is the best image, but it's a scene, not a structure. Keep it as a beat (see Part 4).

## PART 2: The contract (Draft B only)

> You were born into a world where everyone lives to 150. The clock: 150 years. The penalty: at 150 you die, whatever you've saved. Run out before then and you live the leftover years on nothing. The rules: nobody ages much. Nobody retires early. Nobody above you is leaving. The adversary: the meter. Being alive costs $40,000 a year. For 150 years. Six million dollars. It runs whether you work or not.

(78 words)

**Adversary rate:** $40,000 a year, in today's dollars, with no investment growth. Total bill: $40,000 × 150 = $6,000,000.

**Ledger (bill still to pay):**
- Age 0: 6,000,000 − 40,000 × 0 = **$6,000,000**
- Age 20 (your parents paid for these years): 6,000,000 − 40,000 × 20 = **$5,200,000**
- Age 70 (when you'd die in the old world): 6,000,000 − 40,000 × 70 = **$3,200,000**. You've spent a whole old-world life and you still owe more than half.
- Age 120 (retirement): 6,000,000 − 40,000 × 120 = **$1,200,000**. This must already be in the bank.
- Age 150: 6,000,000 − 40,000 × 150 = **$0**

**Side figure:** you have 100 working years (20 to 120) to save the $1,200,000 you need at 120. 1,200,000 ÷ 100 = **$12,000 a year**. If you retire at 65 instead, 45 working years must fund 85 years of retirement: 85 × 40,000 = 3,400,000, and 3,400,000 ÷ 45 = **$75,556 a year**. That is more than the meter costs, so it can't be done.

## PART 3: World kit

**Places**
1. Marrow Street. Forty terraced houses, each front door painted with the year it was bought. None of the years is less than 90 years old.
2. The Greyfield Exchange. A trading floor where the desk nameplates are engraved in brass, not printed, because nobody has moved desks in 80 years.
3. St Aldric's. A crematorium with half of it converted into a nursery. One chimney, one playground, one car park.

**Recurring objects**
1. The family photo. It is re-shot every 20 years, and the frame gets wider each time. Count the rows to read the year.
2. A brass house key with a paper tag of owners' names. For 120 years the tag holds one name.
3. A payslip with a line that reads "Years to retirement: 94".

**Recurring figure**
- A man in his 90s in a sharp suit. He sits outside the same corner office each decade and says only, "Is he in?"

**Absurd details (one per act)**
- Act 1: A card-shop aisle with "Happy 75th — you're halfway!" and "Great-Great-Great-Grandmother's Day" cards.
- Act 2: A 100-year mortgage. The monthly payment is only $295 less than on the 30-year loan. You pay $1.2 million more in interest.
- Act 3: An undertaker's apprentice who has trained for 60 years and has never seen a body.

## PART 4: Third-order consequences

1. **Inheritance.** People inherit later → you inherit your mother's house at about 120, with 30 years left → at 25, heirs sell their future inheritance to a bank for cash today. The bank now owns the right to Mum's house, and it sends her a birthday card every year asking how she's feeling.
   **Test:** it's still a child waiting 120 years for a parent to die.
2. **Work.** People retire later → careers run 100 years, 20 to 120 → a degree earned at 20 is 80 years stale, so a 70-year-old sits in a lecture hall next to an 18-year-old doing the same course. Her CV is 11 pages.
   **Test:** two classmates 52 years apart who both have 80 years of work left.
3. **Top jobs and homes.** The boss stays longer → a boss who holds the job for 40 years instead of 7 means about a sixth as many openings → ambition leaves the old institutions. Every founder is under 40, because only a new company has no queue. Term limits become the young's one political cause. The man outside the corner office is still waiting.
   **Test:** the only way up is out, because nobody above you dies.
4. **Population and family.** More people alive → five generations alive at once → at a six-year-old's birthday party, up to 62 guests are her direct ancestors (2 + 4 + 8 + 16 + 32). She is one of only three children in the room.
   **Test:** the family photo needs five rows.

**Chain I stopped:** the push toward safety leads to insurance pricing and actuarial law. That's a different episode.

## PART 5: The mechanic, verified

**1. Savings compound hard.** $10,000 invested:

| Years | 3% real | 5% real |
|---|---|---|
| 30 | $24,273 | $43,219 |
| 70 | $79,178 | $304,264 |
| 130 | $466,800 | $5,683,000 |

- **Blunt catch:** this does not make everyone rich. If everyone saves for 150-year lives, there's more money chasing the same investments, and real returns likely fall. I'm not sure by how much. Don't show 5% for 130 years as a sure thing.

**2. Debt: long loans barely lower the payment.** A $300,000 loan at 6%:
- 30-year loan: $1,799 a month. Total interest about $347,000.
- 100-year loan: $1,504 a month. Total interest about $1.5 million.
- The interest alone is $1,500 a month, so the payment can never drop below that. Longer loans mostly become interest-only for life.

**3. Inheritance timing.** You inherit at your parent's death age minus their age when you were born.
- Today: 80 − 30 = inherit at about 50.
- At 150: 150 − 30 = inherit at about 120.
- **Honest catch:** as a share of your life, it's similar. Today you inherit 62% of the way through (50 of 80). At 150 it's 80% (120 of 150). Later, but not absurdly so.
- The real change is that the estate has had 150 years to compound. Wealth piles up at the top of long family lines.
- **Unknown you must decide:** whether fertility stretches too. If menopause slows at the same rate, women stay fertile to about 80, the gap between generations can widen, and inheritance ages move.

**4. Population sum.**
- Long-run population = births per year × lifespan.
- Births today are roughly 130 million a year.
- At 73 years of life that gives about 9.5 billion. At 150 years it gives about 19.5 billion, if births hold steady.
- The replacement rate is still about 2.1 children per woman, over a whole lifetime. Living longer doesn't change it. Population stops growing, but at about double today's level.
- Births per 1,000 people fall from about 12.5 to about 6.7.
- Children (under 20) fall from about 25% of people to about 13%.

**5. Work and pensions.** This assumes equal-sized birth years:

| Setup | Workers per retiree |
|---|---|
| Today (work 20–65, die at 80) | 45 ÷ 15 = 3.0 |
| 150-year life, retire at 120 | 100 ÷ 30 = 3.3 |
| 150-year life, retire at 65 | 45 ÷ 85 = 0.53 (collapse) |

- The share of pay you must save (no growth): today 15/60 = 25%. Retire at 120: 30/130 = 23%. Retire at 65: 85/130 = 65%, which is impossible.
- If the pension age moves with lifespan, the system gets slightly healthier.

**6. Homes and top jobs.**
- Homes freed up by an owner's death: today someone owns from about 35 to 80, so 1 in 45 homes a year. At 150 they own from 35 to 150, so 1 in 115 homes a year. That's about 2.5 times slower.
- You'd also need about 1.9 times as many homes.
- Moving house for other reasons isn't necessarily affected. Only the supply freed by deaths dries up.
- Openings = seats ÷ years each person holds the seat. For 500 top seats, 7-year terms give about 71 openings a year. 40-year terms give about 12. Each person's chance falls about sixfold.

**Where the premise is wrong:**

1. **"Die at 150, not 70 extra years old": half right.**
   - If ageing simply slows after 25, old age stretches too.
   - Say body age = 25 + (real age − 25) × 0.45. Then 150 gives body-age 81 and 113 gives body-age 65.
   - So you are "old" from 113 to 150: 37 years, against about 15 today. Not 70 extra years, but about 22.
   - If you want people young until 145 and then dead, that's a second, different premise: decline gets squeezed into the last few years. Pick one and say which.
2. **"Everyone" is ambiguous, and it matters a lot.**
   - If the change slows everyone from the body-age they already have, a 70-year-old gains only about 22 years and dies at about 92. A 40-year-old dies at about 129. Only people under 25 reach 150.
   - So the first 150-year-olds appear about 125 years in, and deaths taper off rather than stopping. That's more believable.
   - If 80-year-olds jump back to healthy, that's rejuvenation, not slowing. Also a different premise.
3. **"Die around 150" isn't guaranteed.**
   - With ageing removed, accidents and violence still kill. At roughly 1 in 1,500 people a year (I'm not sure of the exact figure), about 8% die before 150.
   - Road deaths become the top killer, and a crash at 40 now costs 110 years.
4. **The premise never says whether childhood slows.** I assumed it doesn't: kids grow up in 20 years as now.

## PART 6: The quotable beat

At a six-year-old's birthday party, up to 62 of the guests are her direct ancestors, and her parents won't inherit anything until they're 120.

## PART 7: Where it gets boring

- **Overpopulation panic.** It's been done to death. Show it in one image, not a segment.
- **"Would immortality be boring?"** Philosophy, not mechanics. Viewers leave.
- **"The rich will live longer."** It doesn't apply, because everyone gets this. Don't drift into it.
- **The pension crisis.** Predictable. Only the retire-at-65 twist (0.53 workers per retiree) saves it.
- **Draft B's middle.** Ages 40 to 110 in "you" format can feel like 70 years of the same. It needs a new rung, loan or queue about every 15 years, or the viewer drifts.
- **Tables of numbers without a picture.** Every figure needs its object: the key, the photo, the payslip.

## PART 8: Cascade kit (Draft A)

**The constraint removed:** the world is built on people leaving at about 80. Every 40 years or so, houses, jobs, money and power come free. The premise takes away that release valve.

**Day one:** nothing visible happens. A man blows out 40 candles, same as ever. Across town, an actuary changes one cell in a spreadsheet from 80 to 150, and every cell below it turns red.

**Domains**

1. **Savings**
   - First order: money compounds for 130 years.
   - Second order: everyone saves more, and returns shrink.
   - Image: a savings passbook with 40 filled pages.
2. **Debt**
   - First order: 100-year mortgages appear.
   - Second order: they're interest-only for life, and debt gets passed down like furniture.
   - Image: a mortgage statement showing "Payment 847 of 1,200".
3. **Inheritance**
   - First order: heirs inherit at 120.
   - Second order: heirs sell their future inheritance for cash at 25.
   - Image: the brass key tag with one name for 120 years.
4. **Work**
   - First order: 100-year careers.
   - Second order: you retrain two or three times, so there are 70-year-old students.
   - Image: the 11-page CV.
5. **Homes**
   - First order: fewer homes come free, and about 1.9 times as many are needed.
   - Second order: the young build new towns because the old towns are full.
   - Image: Marrow Street's painted door years.
6. **Power**
   - First order: judges, CEOs and professors sit for 60 years or more.
   - Second order: term limits everywhere.
   - Image: a courthouse wall with six portraits of the same judge, each slightly greyer.
7. **Family and death**
   - First order: five generations alive at once.
   - Second order: children are rare and death is rarer.
   - Image: St Aldric's, half crematorium, half nursery.

**Upside-down domain: health.** Your parents' good health is your bad financial news. Every year Mum stays well is another year you rent.

**The long run (year 100 and beyond):**
- About 17–19 billion people, levelling off. Births are half today's rate.
- Five-row family photos.
- Everyone has three careers.
- Every job has a term limit.
- Houses are held for three centuries.
- The oldest hold most of the wealth.
- Old age still comes, from about 113 to 150.
- The biggest killer is the road.

**Mirror line:** We think a life is a ladder. It was always a queue, and the only thing that moved it was death.
